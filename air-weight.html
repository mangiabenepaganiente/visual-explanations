Visual explanations · gases

Air has weight

Air feels like nothing, but it’s made of molecules, and molecules have mass. At room temperature and sea level a cubic meter of air is about 1.2 kg. The air in an ordinary 10 × 8 × 3 m classroom is about 290 kg, roughly four adults. Pump air into a basketball and the scale goes up by a few grams. The air pressure around you is the weight of the air above, about 10 tonnes on every square meter.

Try thisPress Let air out, then Pump up, and watch the scale. Notice the ball gets about 4–5 g heavier at 8 psi, about the weight of a few paper clips.

Cause 01

Gases are made of molecules with mass

Air is mostly N₂ and O₂. One mole (6×10²³ molecules) of dry air has a mass of 28.96 g. At room conditions a cubic meter holds about 41 moles, which is ≈ 1.2 kg. The molecules are spread out, not missing.

Cause 02

Density from the ideal gas law

ρ = p·M / (R·T). More pressure packs more molecules in, so air is denser at sea level than in the mountains. Warmer air is less dense, which is why hot air rises. The air in a 240 m³ classroom comes to ≈ 289 kg.

Cause 03

Pressure is the weight of the air above

The whole atmosphere rests on the ground. Sea-level pressure 101 kPa = 101 325 N/m² means the air column above each square meter weighs about 10.3 tonnes. You don’t feel crushed because the air pushes on you equally from every side, inside and out.

Myth, busted

“Air weighs nothing”

Air floats in air, so you never feel its weight, just as water feels weightless while you swim in it. Squeeze extra air into a closed ball and the extra mass shows up on a scale. Toggle the myth: it predicts no change on the scale and 0 kg in the room. Both are measurably wrong.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Ideal gas. ρ = p·M/(R·T) with R = 8.314462618 J/(mol·K) and M = 0.0289652 kg/mol (dry air). At 101 325 Pa and 20 °C that gives 1.2041 kg/m³. Humidity lowers density slightly (water vapor, 18 g/mol, is lighter than air), by about 1% for warm, humid air. It is ignored here. At these conditions air behaves as an ideal gas to about 0.1%.
  2. Pressure vs altitude from the standard atmosphere: p = 101325·(1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵·h)5.25588 Pa. Room temperature is set separately, because indoor air is heated or cooled. Weather moves pressure by a few percent.
  3. Room air. Mass = ρ × length × width × height, and weight = mass × g (9.80665 m/s²). The room is treated as empty (furniture and people take up a little of the volume). “Adults” uses 70 kg each.
  4. Basketball. A size-7 ball holds about 7.1 L. Recommended inflation is roughly 7.5–8.5 psi gauge (1 psi = 6894.76 Pa). The extra air mass is Δm = pgauge·V·M/(R·T). The scale reads shell mass plus only this extra air. The ball’s air at outside pressure is exactly canceled by buoyancy, because the ball displaces its own volume of outside air. We compare the same ball, same volume, at 0 psi and pumped. A truly flat, crumpled ball has less volume, which barely changes the answer. The shell mass (600 g) is a typical value; official balls run about 567–650 g.
  5. Air column. Mass above each m² = p/g. That is exact for a hydrostatic atmosphere, with g taken as constant. The molecule picture in the room is illustrative: the dot count scales with density but is not a molecule count (a real room holds about 6×10²⁷ molecules).
  6. For teachers. Matter and its interactions: gases have mass and are made of particles (NGSS 5-PS1-1, MS-PS1-1). It is a classic misconception that gases are “nothing” or weigh less than nothing. A balance-beam demo with two balloons, or a ball weighed before and after pumping on a 0.1 g scale, makes it concrete.
