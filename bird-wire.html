Why birds don’t get zapped on one wire

A power line can sit thousands of volts above the ground, yet a pigeon naps on it. Its feet aren’t rubber. Current only flows through something when there is a voltage difference across it, and both feet sit on the same wire at almost the same voltage. Push 100 A through a typical aluminum line (about 0.1 Ω per km) and two feet 5 cm apart are only about 0.5 millivolts apart, far less than a flashlight battery. Let a wing touch a second wire or the grounded pole, though, and the bird bridges thousands of volts.

Cause 01

Current needs a voltage difference

Ohm’s law, I = ΔV / R, cares about the difference in voltage across something, not how high above the ground it sits. The bird, the wire, and both feet all ride up and down together at about 7,200 V. Being “at high voltage” is harmless. Bridging two different voltages is not.

Cause 02

The wire is a far better path

Between the feet, the bird is in parallel with a few centimeters of thick aluminum, about 10 micro-ohms. The bird’s body is around a thousand ohms. Current splits in inverse proportion to resistance, so roughly 1 part in 100 million takes the bird. The drop between the feet is ΔV = I · Rwire · d.

Cause 03

Two contacts make a path

A wing on a second phase wire puts the full line-to-line voltage across the bird. A wing on a grounded pole or crossarm puts the phase-to-ground voltage across it. Either way the current is amps, not microamps. This is why large birds like hawks and eagles, and squirrels, are electrocuted and cause many power outages.

Myth, busted

“Birds have insulated feet” · “electricity only hurts people”

Bird feet are skin and scale, and they conduct. Rubber feet wouldn’t matter on one wire, because there’s almost no voltage across them anyway. And they wouldn’t help if a wing touches the pole, because the current goes in through the wing. Birds get electrocuted exactly like people do when they bridge two voltages.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Voltage between the feet. The wire between the feet is a resistor Rseg = r · d, with r the conductor resistance per length and d the foot spacing. The bird’s body is in parallel with it, so the bird’s current is Ibird = Iline · Rseg / (Rseg + Rbird). This is effectively ΔV / Rbird, since Rseg is tiny. Line current is RMS AC, and so are the voltages and currents shown.
  2. Conductor resistance (representative, 20 °C DC, rounded). ACSR 1/0 “Raven” ≈ 0.54 Ω/km, ACSR 336.4 kcmil “Linnet” ≈ 0.17 Ω/km, ACSR 795 kcmil “Drake” ≈ 0.072 Ω/km. Real AC resistance at operating temperature is roughly 10–25% higher, which changes nothing here. The lede’s round example uses 0.1 Ω/km.
  3. Bird’s body resistance ≈ 1 kΩ (approximate). This is an order-of-magnitude stand-in. Real values depend on species, foot contact, and wet or dry conditions, and for a dry perched bird they are probably much higher. A higher value only makes the one-wire current smaller.
  4. Bridging. “Wing touches 2nd wire” puts the phase-to-phase voltage across the bird. “Touches the pole” assumes a grounded-wye system and grounded pole hardware, so it bridges VLL/√3. The current shown is V / Rbird. A real contact quickly becomes an arc. The fault current is set by the grid and cut off by fuses or reclosers, which is the outage. Avian-protection guidance (APLIC, Suggested Practices for Avian Protection on Power Lines, 2006) recommends about 150 cm of separation for eagles.
  5. The one current that does flow on one wire. AC lines swing their voltage 60 times a second, so a bird’s body must keep charging and discharging through its feet. Treating the bird as a small sphere (radius 8 cm, C = 4πε₀a ≈ 9 pF) gives I = 2π·60·C·V, about 24 µA on a 12.47 kV distribution line. That’s an order-of-magnitude estimate, and it is still below the 0.5 mA perception level. On the highest-voltage transmission lines it grows toward that level, and corona discharge appears.
  6. Danger bands are the human 60 Hz body-current zones from IEC 60479-1, shown only for scale: under ~0.5 mA not felt, ~0.5–10 mA felt, ~10–50 mA muscle lock, above ~50 mA the risk of heart fibrillation, and amps cause burns. Birds differ in detail but not in principle.
  7. Safety. Never touch, climb toward, or fly anything near a power line, and stay far away from downed wires. A person on a ladder or the ground is the “pole” in this picture. Their feet are grounded, so any contact puts the full phase voltage across the body.
  8. For teachers. Covers potential difference vs. potential, Ohm’s law, parallel paths, and current division (NGSS HS-PS3-5, MS-PS2-3; UK KS3/KS4 potential difference). The misconception to name is “high voltage alone shocks you” or “birds are insulated”.
