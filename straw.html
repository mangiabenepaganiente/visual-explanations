How a straw really works

It feels like you pull the drink up. You don’t: a vacuum can’t pull anything. When you lower the pressure in your mouth, the air pressing on the drink’s surface is left unbalanced and pushes the liquid up the straw. It rises until the column’s weight balances the difference: h = ΔP/(ρ·g). Even a perfect vacuum lifts water only about 10.3 m, and on the airless Moon a straw lifts nothing at all.

Cause 01

Air is already pushing on the drink

At sea level the air presses on every square meter of the drink with about 101 kPa, roughly 10 tonnes-force. With the straw open to the air, that push acts equally inside and outside the straw, so nothing moves.

Cause 02

Your mouth removes push from above

Your cheeks and tongue enlarge your mouth, which lowers the pressure in it by ΔP. Now the air outside pushes harder on the drink than the mouth air pushes down the straw. The difference shoves liquid up the tube.

Cause 03

The column stops at h = ΔP/ρg

Liquid rises until the column’s own weight per area, ρ·g·h, equals ΔP. A 20 cm straw of water needs only ~2 kPa. A perfect vacuum (ΔP = full air pressure) caps water at 101325/(1000·9.81) ≈ 10.3 m. That’s why old suction pumps couldn’t raise water from deeper wells.

Myth, busted

“You suck the liquid up”

“Suction” isn’t a pulling force. Low pressure can’t grab a liquid. If it could, sucking harder would lift water any height, and a straw would work on the Moon. Toggle the myth: it predicts the red column. Reality: the lift is capped by the outside air pressure, and with no air it is zero.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Hydrostatics. In a still liquid column, pressure falls with height as p(z) = pout − ρ·g·z. The column stops where that equals the mouth pressure: h = ΔP/(ρ·g), with ΔP = pout − pmouth. Mouth pressure can’t drop below zero, so ΔP ≤ pout and hmax = pout/(ρ·g). g = 9.80665 m/s² on Earth and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon.
  2. Outside air pressure: sea level 101.325 kPa (standard atmosphere). Denver (1609 m) 83.4 kPa (standard atmosphere). Everest summit 33.7 kPa, the measured typical value, which is a little higher than the standard-atmosphere 31.4 kPa. The Moon’s surface pressure is about 10−10 Pa, effectively zero. Weather shifts all of these by a few percent.
  3. Densities (approximate, room temperature): water 1000 kg/m³ (998 at 20 °C), orange juice ≈ 1045, milkshake ≈ 1100 (varies with recipe), mercury 13 595 (0 °C, the standard barometer value that gives 760 mm). Density sets the height. A thick milkshake is hard to drink mostly because of viscosity, which slows the flow. This static model doesn’t include viscosity. The animation eases toward the equilibrium height and is not a flow simulation.
  4. The real ceiling is a little lower. Water boils when its pressure drops to its vapor pressure (≈ 2.3 kPa at 20 °C), so a real vacuum pump lifts cold water about 10.1 m, not 10.3 m. The straw’s own capillary rise (a millimeter or so for a drinking straw) is ignored.
  5. Human suction. Sipping through a normal straw takes only a couple of kPa. Strong, sustained mouth suction is roughly 10–20 kPa. Published maximums vary widely with method and person, so treat the upper range as approximate. That is why a few-meter straw is already very hard to drink from.
  6. Drawing. Heights are to scale vertically. The cup, straw width, and face are drawn at a fixed readable size.
  7. For teachers. Pressure and forces (NGSS MS-PS2-2, HS-PS2-1). Related demos: the collapsing can, Torricelli’s barometer, and the 10 m limit on suction pumps that puzzled Galileo’s era. The key idea: fluids are pushed from high pressure toward low. “Suction” is the name for removing a push, not adding a pull.
