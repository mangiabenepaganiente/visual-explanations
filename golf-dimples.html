Why golf balls have dimples

It seems like a smooth ball should slip through the air more easily. At golf speeds it doesn’t. Air flowing over a smooth ball peels away early, about 80° around from the front, leaving a wide, low-pressure wake that drags the ball back. Dimples stir the thin layer of air touching the ball into turbulence. Turbulent air carries more momentum, so it clings until about 120° before letting go. The wake shrinks and the drag coefficient roughly halves, from about 0.5 to 0.26. A drive that carries about 245 m here would land near 100 m if the ball were smooth, and under 140 m even at the smooth ball’s best angle.

Cause 01

The wake is the drag

At golf speeds almost all of a ball’s drag is pressure drag. Air at the front is squeezed to high pressure, and the region behind the ball is a low-pressure tumble of eddies. That pressure difference pushes the ball backward. A wider wake means more drag. Surface friction itself is a small part.

Cause 02

Turbulence clings longer

The thin boundary layer on a smooth ball stays smooth (laminar) and runs out of momentum on the back side, so it separates near 80°. Dimples trip it into turbulence, which keeps mixing in fast-moving outer air. It survives the rising pressure on the back and separates near 120°. That shift is the drag crisis: Cd drops from about 0.5 to about 0.25.

Cause 03

Dimples move the crisis to golf speeds

A smooth sphere has its own drag crisis, but only above Re ≈ 3–4 × 10⁵, faster than any drive. With D = 42.7 mm, a 70 m/s ball sits at Re = vD/ν ≈ 2.0 × 10⁵. Dimples trigger the switch already near 5 × 10⁴, so the ball has low drag for almost its whole flight. Dimples also make backspin lift strong and reliable.

Myth, busted

“A smooth ball is more aerodynamic”

Smooth wins only for skin friction, and that barely matters for a ball. Toggle the myth to see the flight it predicts (red dotted): a smooth ball with low drag and the same spin, outflying the dimpled one. In real wind-tunnel data the smooth ball has about twice the drag at these speeds and lands far shorter. Golfers found this out in the 1800s, when scuffed, hand-hammered balls flew farther than new smooth ones.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Ball and air. Diameter 42.67 mm and mass 45.93 g (the Rules of Golf minimum size and maximum mass). Air density ρ = 1.204 kg/m³ and kinematic viscosity ν ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s (about 20 °C, sea level). Re = vD/ν, drag F = ½ρv²A·Cd, and lift ½ρv²A·CL perpendicular to the velocity.
  2. Drag coefficients are representative curves fitted to wind-tunnel literature, not one specific ball. Smooth sphere: Cd ≈ 0.5 across the subcritical range, with its drag crisis near Re ≈ 3.5 × 10⁵ (Achenbach 1972, J. Fluid Mech. 54:565). Dimpled ball: drag crisis near Re ≈ 5 × 10⁴, then Cd ≈ 0.23 rising slowly to about 0.27 at driver speeds (Bearman & Harvey 1976, “Golf ball aerodynamics”, Aeronautical Quarterly 27:112). Real balls differ by dimple design by roughly ±10%.
  3. Backspin lift (approximate). For the dimpled ball CL ≈ 0.54·S0.4, with spin ratio S = ωr/v, capped at 0.35. This is a fit reported by Smits & Smith (1994) and gives about 0.2 at launch for a 2,700 rpm drive. Spin decays with a 20 s time constant. Spin also adds some drag, which is ignored here. Smooth spinning spheres give weak and sometimes even reversed lift (inverse Magnus effect), so the smooth ball is flown without lift. That is a simplification, and it is why the drag-only dimpled path (dashed) is also shown for a fair comparison.
  4. Trajectory. 2D RK4 integration with a 5 ms step, Cd re-evaluated from the instantaneous Re, no wind, flat ground, carry measured to the landing point (no roll). As a check, a tour-average drive (74.7 m/s, 10.9°, 2,686 rpm, from TrackMan PGA Tour averages) carries about 260 m in this model, against about 250 m measured.
  5. Wake picture is schematic. Streamlines use ideal flow around a circle up to the separation angle. Separation angle goes from about 80° (laminar) to about 120° (turbulent) in step with Cd. Wake width is drawn from that angle. The real flow is three-dimensional and unsteady, and the wake sheds vortices.
  6. For teachers. Covers forces and motion, drag, and projectile motion with air resistance (NGSS HS-PS2-1, HS-ETS1-2). The counterintuitive design lesson is that roughness can reduce drag. Pair with a vacuum check: with Cd = 0 the model returns the textbook range v²·sin 2θ / g.
