Why batteries don’t store electricity

It’s natural to picture a battery as a tank of electrons that drains into the bulb. It isn’t. The wires are already full of mobile electrons. A battery is a chemical energy pump: its reactions push those electrons around the loop, and every electron that leaves one terminal is matched by one arriving at the other. So the battery’s net charge stays zero. What runs out is chemical energy, about 3.75 Wh in an AA cell.

Cause 01

The charges are already there

Copper has about 8.5×10²⁸ free electrons per cubic meter. Just 30 cm of ordinary hook-up wire holds about 10²² of them before you connect anything. Close the switch and they all start drifting together, like a bike chain. Nothing has to travel from the battery to the bulb first.

Cause 02

The battery is a pump, not a tank

Chemical reactions at the two electrodes push electrons out of the − terminal and take the same number back in at +, every second. Each coulomb gets V joules of energy (1.5 J for an AA), which it gives up in the bulb. Net charge in the battery stays zero.

Cause 03

mAh counts charge pumped, Wh counts energy

“2500 mAh” means the cell can push 2.5 A for an hour (9000 C) through the circuit before its chemicals run out. The stored thing is energy: 2.5 Ah × 1.5 V = 3.75 Wh = 13.5 kJ. Drain it hard and less is usable.

Myth, busted

“A battery is a tank of electrons that empties into the bulb”

If it were, the bulb and wires would fill with extra electrons. 9000 C piled on a bulb would put it at an absurd ~10¹⁵ volts, and a “dead” battery would be strongly charged. Neither happens. A dead battery has the same electrons it started with. Its chemicals are just used up. Toggle the myth to see the tank picture fail.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Circuit. An ideal EMF with internal resistance r driving a resistive load R: I = ε/(R + r). Power to the load is I²R, heat inside the battery is I²r, and the chemical energy used is ε·I·t, their sum. The validator checks this energy balance. Electrons per second = I/e, with e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C.
  2. Battery presets (typical, approximate): AA alkaline ε 1.5 V, r ≈ 0.15 Ω, 2500 mAh at light drain. 9 V alkaline ε 9 V, r ≈ 1.7 Ω, 550 mAh. Li-ion 18650 ε 3.7 V (nominal), r ≈ 0.05 Ω, 3000 mAh. “Chemical energy” is nominal voltage × rated capacity: 3.75, 4.95 and 11.1 Wh.
  3. Capacity depends on drain. Alkaline cells deliver far less charge at high current. We use a Peukert-style curve, Ceff = C·(Iref/I)k−1 above Iref. AA: Iref 100 mA, k 1.3. 9 V: 25 mA, 1.2. Li-ion: 600 mA, 1.05. This is a rough fit to typical datasheet curves (an AA gives about 1.2–1.5 Ah at 0.5–1 A). Real capacity also depends on cutoff voltage, temperature and rest periods.
  4. Constant voltage simplification. We hold ε fixed until empty. A real alkaline cell sags from about 1.6 V to about 0.9 V as it discharges, and Li-ion goes from 4.2 V to 3.0 V. We also skip self-discharge.
  5. Drift speed v = I/(n·A·e) with n = 8.49×10²⁸ m⁻³ for copper and a 0.5 mm² wire. Electrons crawl at fractions of a millimeter per second, but the electric field that sets them all moving spreads through the circuit at a large fraction of light speed. That’s why the bulb lights instantly.
