Why a broken circuit kills the bulb

A battery doesn’t squirt electricity into a bulb to be used up. The wires and the filament are already full of free electrons. The battery pushes on all of them at once, but only if there is an unbroken loop from one terminal back to the other. Then the same current flows into the bulb and out of it; what the bulb takes is energy, not charge. Cut the loop anywhere and the current stops everywhere, almost instantly.

Cause 01

Charge goes round, it isn’t used up

Charge is conserved and doesn’t pile up in a wire, so whatever flows into a bulb flows out of it. In a single loop the current is the same at every point: I = V / Rtotal. What the bulb takes is energy: each coulomb arrives with more volts than it leaves with, and the difference lights the filament.

Cause 02

No loop, no push

At a gap the electrons have nowhere to go. A tiny bit of charge piles up at the cut ends in about a nanosecond, and its field cancels the battery’s push inside the wire. The whole battery voltage then appears across the gap, and the current everywhere else is zero, including right next to the battery.

Cause 03

Fast signal, slow electrons

Flip the switch and the push travels as an electromagnetic field at a large fraction of light speed, reaching a bulb in nanoseconds. The electrons themselves only drift at a fraction of a millimetre per second: vd = I/(n·A·e). The bulb lights at once because the filament’s own electrons start moving.

Myth, busted

“The bulb uses up current” · “one wire is enough”

If bulbs used up current, the ammeter after a bulb would read less than the one before it, and a second bulb in series would glow dimmer than the first. Neither happens. And with only one wire (any cut), nothing lights. Turn on myth mode to see the “used up” readings the myth predicts next to the real ones.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Ideal DC circuit. An ideal battery (no internal resistance), zero-resistance wires and ideal ammeters, solved with Ohm’s law and Kirchhoff’s rules. Single bulb I = V/R₁; series I = V/(R₁ + R₂), V₁ = I·R₁, V₁ + V₂ = V; parallel I₁ = V/R₁, I₂ = V/R₂, main current I₁ + I₂. Real batteries have internal resistance (so adding a parallel bulb dims the others slightly) and real wires have a little resistance.
  2. Bulbs are treated as fixed resistors. A real tungsten filament’s resistance is roughly 10–15× higher hot than cold, so its current is not strictly proportional to voltage, and there is an inrush surge at switch-on. Treat each slider value as the hot, glowing resistance (a 6 V, 3 W flashlight bulb is about 12 Ω hot). Brightness is drawn from power P = I²R.
  3. Drift speed. vd = I/(n·A·e) with n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³ for copper (one free electron per atom), e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. Check: 1 A in 1 mm² gives 1/(8.5×10²⁸ × 10⁻⁶ × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 7.3×10⁻⁵ m/s, about 0.07 mm/s. On top of this slow drift, the electrons also move randomly at ~10⁶ m/s (Fermi speed), which the drawing leaves out. The drawn dots are sped up enormously so you can see the flow.
  4. How fast does the bulb react? The change in the field that pushes the electrons travels along the wires as an electromagnetic wave, at a sizeable fraction of c (about ⅔ c in typical cables), so a 1 m loop settles in a few nanoseconds (a little longer as the surface charges rearrange). The page shows this as instant.
  5. Electrons vs conventional current. Dots are electrons, which drift from the battery’s − terminal around to its + terminal. Conventional current (arrows, what ammeters report) is defined the other way. Both describe the same flow.
  6. Myth readings. The red “used up” numbers are not physics; they show what an ammeter would read if each bulb consumed half the current passing through it. Classroom ammeters show the real (equal) readings.
  7. For teachers. Electric circuits, energy transfer and conservation (NGSS MS-PS2-3, MS-PS3-5, HS-PS3-1; UK KS3 current and potential difference). Name the misconceptions: “current is used up by the bulb”, “one wire from the battery is enough”, and “electrons race from the battery to the bulb.” Classic check: put ammeters on both sides of a bulb.
