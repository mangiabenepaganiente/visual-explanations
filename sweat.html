Sweat cools you by leaving

Sweat is usually as warm as your skin when it appears. Cooling happens when the fastest molecules escape into vapor — each kilogram that leaves carries away about 2430 kJ of latent heat near skin temperature. Sitting wet without evaporating barely cools you; humidity and still air prove it.

Cause 01

Leaving takes heat

Evaporation is a phase change. Breaking liquid bonds costs energy — about 2430 kJ per kg near 30 °C (vs ~2260 at 100 °C). Cooling power is P ≈ ṁ · L_v. The liquid that stays behind is cooler because the hottest, fastest molecules left.

Cause 02

Humidity raises the ceiling

Net flux needs a vapor-pressure gap: e_skin − e_air. Relative humidity sets e_air = RH · e_sat(T_air). At RH = 100% with air as warm as skin, the gap collapses and net evaporation (and cooling) goes to zero.

Cause 03

Wind thins the boundary

Moving air raises the convective mass-transfer coefficient. More wind → larger h_e → more watts for the same vapor-pressure gap. A fan does not cool the room; it helps sweat leave your skin.

Myth, busted

Not “wet = cold liquid”

Sweat emerges near skin temperature. Feeling cool when wet is mostly evaporative cooling (and wetness cues your brain reads as cold). Toggle the myth chip: same warm liquid, no escape — almost no cooling.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Latent heat. Empirical linear fit used in meteorology and psychrometry: L_v(T) = 2.501×10⁶ − 2361·T J/kg with T in °C (valid for liquid water near ambient). Gives L_v(30 °C) ≈ 2.430×10⁶ J/kg = 2430 kJ/kg and L_v(100 °C) ≈ 2.265×10⁶ ≈ 2260 kJ/kg — matching steam-table order of magnitude. Cooling power P = ṁ · L_v.
  2. Saturation vapor pressure (Magnus–Tetens). e_sat(T) = 610.94 · exp(17.625·T / (T + 243.04)) Pa, T in °C (Alduchov & Eskridge 1996 form). Air vapor pressure e_air = RH · e_sat(T_air). Wet-skin vapor pressure for a freely sweating patch is taken as e_skin = e_sat(T_skin) (wetted fraction = 1 on the drawn area).
  3. Evaporative heat flux (Lewis / ASHRAE-style). Forced-convection heat-transfer coefficient h_c = max(3.5, 8.3·v0.6) W/(m²·K) with wind v in m/s (still-air floor 3.5). Lewis relation at sea level: h_e = 16.5 · h_c W/(m²·kPa). Evaporative cooling on area A: E = A · h_e · max(0, p_sk − p_a) watts with pressures in kPa. Then ṁ = E / L_v. This is a published classroom / comfort-engineering simplification (Fanger / ISO 7730 / ASHRAE Handbook lineage), not a full multi-layer skin model.
  4. Patch area & ΔT sketch. The live “cooling watts” use A = 0.10 m² (forearm-scale wet patch) so classroom numbers stay readable. The ΔT sketch is a lumped first-order lag: skin thermal capacity C ≈ 4×10³ J/K for that patch, restoring dry convection toward air temperature, plus evaporative sink −E. It is qualitative — not a measured thermocouple.
  5. Myth mode. “wet = cold liquid” freezes evaporation (forces E = 0) while keeping a warm liquid film on the canvas. You still look wet; cooling collapses. Real still-air wet skin can also exchange heat by ordinary convection/radiation; those channels are not the sweat story.
  6. What we omit. Clothing vapor resistance, salt in sweat (slightly lowers vapor pressure), metabolic heat, radiation, and condensation when e_air > e_skin (clamped to zero cooling, not heating). Whole-body sweating rates (~0.5–2 L/h in heat) scale with larger area; the physics is the same.
  7. For teachers. Phase change, latent heat, humidity, and heat-safety / PE weather. Pairs with wet-bulb demos and “why a fan helps.” NGSS MS-PS3 energy; health standards on heat illness.
