Why ketchup won’t come out, then suddenly does

Honey is thick, but tip it and it always flows, just slowly. Ketchup is different. It has a yield stress: below a certain push (very roughly 15–35 Pa) it holds its shape like a soft solid and doesn’t flow at all. Push past that point and it becomes shear-thinning: the faster it flows, the runnier it gets, so its viscosity can drop a thousandfold. That’s why nothing comes out, and then everything does.

Cause 01

Below the yield stress, it’s a soft solid

Ketchup is a mesh of tomato particles and fibers in liquid. That loose network can carry a small stress without flowing, about τy ≈ 30 Pa here. In a narrow neck, the ketchup’s own weight only pushes on the walls with τw = ρ·a·R/2 ≈ 28 Pa, so it just sits there.

Cause 02

A tap briefly multiplies gravity

Smacking the bottle’s base stops the glass suddenly while the ketchup keeps going, so for a split second it feels several g. The wall stress jumps past yield, the network breaks, and the ketchup in the neck starts to slide.

Cause 03

Then it gets runnier the faster it moves

Once it flows, stress follows τ = τy + K·γ̇n with n ≈ 0.25. The apparent viscosity τ/γ̇ falls steeply as the shear rate γ̇ rises, from “solid” to only about ten times water. A little more push gives far more flow, so it comes out all at once.

Myth, busted

“Ketchup is just thick, like honey”

A honey-thick liquid (≈ 10 Pa·s) would ooze out steadily the moment you tip the bottle past sideways, and a tap would only speed it up a little. Ketchup does nothing, then gushes. Toggle the myth to see the honey prediction. Trapped air matters too, but air can’t get in partly because of the yield stress.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Herschel–Bulkley fluid. τ = τy + K·γ̇n when τ > τy, otherwise no flow. We use representative ketchup values τy = 30 Pa, K = 10 Pa·sn, n = 0.25, density 1140 kg/m³. Published measurements span roughly τy 15–35 Pa (some methods give more), K 5–20 Pa·sn, n 0.2–0.3. They depend on brand, temperature and how the test is done, so treat these as approximate. Apparent viscosity is η = τ/γ̇ = τy/γ̇ + K·γ̇n−1, which falls as γ̇ rises.
  2. Neck plug. A force balance on a cylinder of ketchup of radius R in the neck, driven by acceleration a along the bottle axis, gives wall stress τw = ρ·a·R/2, independent of the plug’s length. We use a narrow neck, R = 5 mm, so gravity alone (28 Pa upside down) sits just below yield. Real bottles live near this edge, which is why the outcome feels so unpredictable. Tilt counts only the component of g along the bottle (a = −g·cos(tilt)). With the neck pointing up or sideways (tilt ≤ 90°) we assume the ketchup has settled away from the opening, so nothing pours even if you tap. We assume the ketchup above the neck is held up by the trapped air at the base of the bottle, so only the plug matters for starting.
  3. Flow rate is the exact Herschel–Bulkley pipe-flow integral, Q = π·R³/τw³ ∫ τ²·γ̇(τ) dτ from τy to τw, evaluated with Simpson’s rule. With τy = 0 and n = 1 it reduces to Poiseuille’s law, and the validator checks this. Taps are 60 ms half-sine pulses. Shaking is a 3 Hz sine. Both are idealized.
  4. Left out. Real ketchup is also thixotropic (shaking weakens its structure for a while, so it flows more easily right after), slips on a thin watery layer at the glass wall, and lets air in through glugs. All of these make real pours even more sudden. When nearly empty (under 15%), we treat the ketchup as a thinner layer clinging to the walls, so the effective R shrinks and it’s harder to get out.
  5. The “57” tap. Heinz has promoted tapping the raised “57” on the neck of its glass bottle. Treat that as folklore with a grain of sense: a firm tap near the neck shakes the ketchup that has to yield, but any sharp tap that pushes the stress past yield works.
  6. Plastic squeeze bottles sidestep the problem: squeezing adds pressure far above yield, and a slit valve keeps the neck full of ketchup.
  7. For teachers. Forces and stress (HS-PS2-1), properties of matter (MS-PS1). Contrast Newtonian fluids (water, honey, oil) with non-Newtonian ones: ketchup and toothpaste are shear-thinning with a yield stress, while cornstarch slime is shear-thickening.
