Why stars twinkle but planets don’t

The air jiggles all starlight. A star is an unresolved point, so one ray bundle dances. A planet is a tiny disk — many independent paths, and the wiggles average out. Closer is not the mechanism. Closer just makes a larger disk.

Cause 01

Air cells

The troposphere is a stack of warm and cool pockets. Each pocket has a slightly different refractive index, so a ray is steered a fraction of an arcsecond. That steering changes in time. Astronomers call the blur seeing and the flicker scintillation.

Cause 02

Point versus disk

A star is far smaller than the seeing disk (~1″). One bundle, one dance. A planet is an angular disk of a few to tens of arcseconds, so many independent paths share the pupil. Twinkle amplitude falls as ~1/√N.

Cause 03

No air, no twinkle

From the ISS — or any orbit above the air — neither stars nor planets twinkle. Hubble’s advantage is not “being closer to the stars.” It is sitting above the phase screen.

Myth, busted

Not “because they’re closer”

Distance is not the mechanism. Closer just makes a larger disk, and the disk is what averages the air. Brightness is a correlation, not a cause: Sirius still winks; Uranus (a dim disk) is steadier than a star of the same brightness.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. What twinkling is. Atmospheric scintillation: turbulent refractive-index fluctuations steer and focus starlight. Image motion is the tip-tilt of the wavefront; intensity flicker is the focusing/defocusing (related to the Laplacian of the phase). Seeing FWHM is the long-exposure blur, typically ~1″ at a decent site. Kolmogorov turbulence uses a phase structure function D_φ(ρ) = 6.88 (ρ/r₀)^{5/3} and Fried parameter r₀ with θ_see ≈ 0.98 λ / r₀. This page does not integrate a 3D Kolmogorov volume.
  2. Disk-averaging model (named formulas). Independent-path count N = 1 + (θ / θ_see)². Point-source twinkle index (RMS of I/⟨I⟩ − 1) T_★ = 0.25 · (θ_see / 1″) when the atmosphere is on, else 0. Extended-source index T = T_★ / √N. Normalized intensity variance σ_I² / ⟨I⟩² = T². Image-motion RMS 0.4 · θ_see / √N arcsec. These are classroom scalings, not a Rytov-variance or isoplanatic-angle calculation. The 0.25 at 1″ is a typical amateur-zenith order of magnitude, not a site forecast.
  3. Phase screen is an illustration. The drawn air cells and ray kicks come from a sum of moving sinusoids / Gaussian-like phase, labeled as such. A real atmosphere is a thick, evolving Kolmogorov cascade with an inner scale and an outer scale. Colors (warm/cool) stand for δn sign, not thermometer readings. Time is sped up so the dance is visible; real scintillation timescales are often ~10 ms.
  4. Angular sizes (apparent diameter). Unresolved star on this page: 0.01″ (most stars are milliarcseconds; Betelgeuse is ~0.05″). Mercury ~4.5–13″; Venus ~10–66″; Mars ~3.5–25″; Jupiter ~30–50″; Saturn ~15–20″ (globe); Uranus ~3.5″; Neptune ~2.3″. Presets use 0.01 / 8 / 40 / 18 / 25. Distance sets θ ≈ 2R/D — that is the only honest link from “closer” to “doesn’t twinkle.”
  5. Planets can twinkle. A small disk (Mars at aphelion, Uranus, Mercury) under poor seeing or a long air mass near the horizon still flickers. The slider will show it: drop θ toward 1″ or raise seeing and watch T rise. The schoolyard line “planets never twinkle” is a useful first cut, not a law.
  6. Brightness is not the cause. Sirius (−1.5 mag) twinkles. Venus (brighter than any star) usually does not, because it is a disk. A dim star still winks; a dim planet is steadier than a star of equal flux. The mechanism is angular coherence, not photon count.
  7. View from ISS. Toggle sets atmosphereOn = false. Then T = 0, intensity variance is 0, and rays are straight. Hubble / JWST / any orbit above the tropopause sees the same: no scintillation. (Space telescopes still have diffraction, jitter, and optical errors — not twinkling.)
  8. Drawings are not maps. Cell size, ray bend, and planet scale are exaggerated so a classroom can see them. The eyepiece maps arcseconds to pixels; the star’s instantaneous blob is a schematic PSF, not an Airy fit. Live intensities on the chart are an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with RMS equal to T, so the plotted flicker matches the readout.
