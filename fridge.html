Your fridge doesn’t make cold

A refrigerator is a heat pump. The vapor-compression cycle moves heat from the cold box to the warm room and adds compressor work W, so Qh = Qc + W. Opening the door does not cool the kitchen — it short-circuits the machine and dumps that heat (plus motor heat) into the room.

Cause 01

Heat is moved, not deleted

The evaporator absorbs Qc from the food compartment. The condenser rejects Qh into the kitchen. Cold is the absence of that heat in the box — not a substance the fridge manufactures.

Cause 02

Work makes Qh bigger

The compressor adds electrical work W. Energy balance on the cycle requires Qh = Qc + W. The coils always dump more heat than the box removes.

Cause 03

COP and the Carnot bound

Cooling COP = Qc / W. An ideal Carnot fridge cannot beat Tc / (Th − Tc) with absolute temperatures. Real machines sit well below that ceiling.

Myth, busted

“Makes cold” / cools the room

Open the door and you feel cold air spill out — but the condenser still rejects Qc + W into the same room. Net kitchen heat rises with W (and more when the compressor works harder). A fridge is not an air conditioner for the kitchen.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Idealized vapor-compression toy. The page shows evaporator (cold), compressor (work in), and condenser coils (hot) with live heat arrows. It is a teaching schematic — not a pressure–enthalpy Mollier chart, not a throttling-valve animation, and not a chemical model of the refrigerant.
  2. Powers. With the compressor on, cooling power is a fixed classroom base Qc = 100 W (door closed) or 150 W (door open — larger load). Work is W = Qc / COP with COP = η · COPCarnot. Then Qh = Qc + W by construction. Units are watts (energy per time).
  3. Carnot COP. For a fridge between cold and hot reservoirs, the reversible upper bound is COPCarnot = Tc / (Th − Tc) with absolute temperatures in kelvin. Cited in every undergrad thermo text (e.g. Cengel & Boles; Fermi). It is an ideal ceiling, not a claim about your kitchen appliance.
  4. η slider. Default η = 0.40 (40% of Carnot) is a round classroom stand-in. Real domestic fridges often land roughly in that ballpark depending on Tc, Th, and age — real COP is lower than Carnot, and the page says so on purpose.
  5. Open door → room warms. In a sealed kitchen, steady energy bookkeeping says net heat to the room equals compressor work W (door closed: Qc leaked in from the room and returned as part of Qh; door open: evaporator also pulls from room air, condenser returns Qc+W). Opening the door raises load → larger W → faster room warming. Feeling cold air at shin height is not whole-room cooling.
  6. Room thermometer. A toy thermal mass integrates net power; it is not a CFD room model. Reset returns the dial to the Th setpoint. Insulation leak when the door is closed is implied by the steady Qc, not drawn as a separate conduction equation.
  7. For teachers. Heat, work, and energy conservation (NGSS MS-PS3); pairs with “why is the back of the fridge warm?” lab demos. Name the misconception out loud: fridges make cold / an open fridge cools the kitchen.
