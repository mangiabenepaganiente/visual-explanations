Sound is a vibration, not flying air

When a speaker plays, no air travels from it to your ear. Each air molecule is nudged forward, pushes its neighbours, and swings back, moving far less than the width of a hair. What travels is the pattern: a chain of squeezed (compression) and stretched (rarefaction) regions that runs through the air at about 343 m/s. Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave, and with no molecules to push, as in a vacuum, there is no sound at all.

Cause 01

A push handed along

The speaker cone shoves the layer of air in front of it. That layer squeezes the next, which squeezes the next. Each molecule returns to where it started as the cone pulls back, so the air as a whole goes nowhere. Only the push (energy) is passed along.

Cause 02

Longitudinal: back and forth along the path

The molecules move along the direction the sound travels, not up and down like a rope wave. Where they bunch up, pressure is a little higher (compression); where they spread out, a little lower (rarefaction). Even a loud 94 dB tone changes air pressure by only 1 Pa, about one part in 100,000.

Cause 03

The medium sets the speed

Wave speed depends on how stiff and how dense the medium is: c = √(K/ρ). Water is much stiffer than air, and steel stiffer still, so sound runs at ~1,480 and ~5,960 m/s. The frequency comes from the source, so the wavelength λ = c/f stretches in faster media.

Myth, busted

“Sound is air travelling to your ear”

If the air itself had to travel, a word spoken across a room would come with a 1,236 km/h wind. Instead the molecules move micrometres or less, and far quieter sounds move them less than the width of an atom. Model, constants, caveats

  1. Plane progressive wave, linear acoustics. Displacement ξ(x,t) = ξ₀·sin(ωt − kx), pressure p = ρc·∂ξ/∂t, so p = ρ·c·u and ξ = p/(ρ·c·ω), with ω = 2πf, k = ω/c, λ = c/f. Real sources spread out, so the amplitude falls with distance; this is the idealized single plane wave. Above roughly 140–150 dB non-linear effects begin to matter and the linear formulas stop being accurate.
  2. Decibels. prms = 20 µPa × 10dB/20 (the airborne reference). So 94 dB is 1.00 Pa rms. Displacement and velocity readouts are rms values; peak values are √2 larger (shown as sub-readings). Underwater acoustics normally quotes dB re 1 µPa instead, which is 26 dB higher for the same pressure. Here every medium uses the same 20 µPa reference so the same pressure is compared across media.
  3. Check. Air at 20 °C: ρ = 1.204 kg/m³, c = 343.2 m/s, ρc ≈ 413 Pa·s/m. At 94 dB and 1 kHz: ξ = 1.00/(413 × 6283) ≈ 3.9×10⁻⁷ m = 0.39 µm rms, u = p/ρc ≈ 2.4 mm/s, λ = 0.343 m. At 0 dB it is about 8 pm, smaller than an atom (~0.1–0.3 nm).
  4. Media. Water (20 °C): ρ = 998 kg/m³, c ≈ 1,482 m/s. Steel: ρ = 7,850 kg/m³, longitudinal bulk-wave speed ≈ 5,960 m/s (a thin rod is slower, ≈ 5,100 m/s, and solids also carry slower shear waves, not shown). The same pressure moves molecules far less in dense, stiff media because ρc is thousands of times larger.
  5. Thermal motion is not shown. Real air molecules already dart about randomly at ~470 m/s and collide every ~70 nm. Sound is a tiny, organized drift on top of that chaos, far smaller than the random motion, which is why it takes collisions to pass it along and why sound speed is similar to molecular speeds.
  6. Drawing. The window auto-zooms to three wavelengths; motion is slowed to about 0.6 cycles per second and displacement is exaggerated (the factor is shown on the canvas). The tone is a sine synthesized only after you click; your speaker’s real loudness is not the SPL on the slider.
  7. For teachers. Waves and their properties; mechanical waves need a medium (NGSS MS-PS4-1, MS-PS4-2, HS-PS4-1). Name the misconception: “sound is air (or a thing) travelling to the ear.” Classic demos: a slinky pushed end-on, or the bell in a jar as the air is pumped out.
