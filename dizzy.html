Why you stay dizzy after you stop spinning

Each inner ear has three fluid-filled loops called the semicircular canals. When your head starts to turn, the fluid inside (endolymph) lags behind, like coffee in a cup you twist. It bends a jelly flap across the canal, the cupula, and hair cells in the flap report “turning.” Keep spinning at a steady rate and the flap springs back within seconds, so the signal fades and you feel almost still. Then you stop. The fluid keeps going and bends the flap the other way, so your ears report spin in the opposite direction while your eyes say you’re standing still.

Cause 01

The fluid lags, the flap bends

The canal walls turn with your skull at once. The endolymph inside is held back by its own inertia, so relative to the canal it flows backward and pushes on the cupula. Hair cells in the cupula turn that bend into nerve signals. The canals sense changes in spin, that is, angular acceleration, and report it as rotation speed.

Cause 02

Steady spin fades away

The cupula is springy, so during a steady spin it relaxes back over a few seconds (time constant ≈ 5–7 s). The brain stretches that out to about 15–20 s with a circuit called velocity storage. Mathematically it’s a high-pass filter: a step in spin rate gives a felt rotation of ω₀·e−t/τ. After 30 s, you hardly feel the spin.

Cause 03

Stop, and the signal flips

Stopping is a sudden change of −ω₀. The fluid keeps going, the flap bends the other way, and you feel −ω₀·e−t/τ: turning backward. Your eyes jerk in a reflex rhythm called nystagmus as they try to track that false spin. Your eyes, which see a still room, disagree with your ears. That conflict is the dizzy, sick feeling.

Myth, busted

“Your brain is sloshing around” · “it’s blood rushing”

Your brain is firmly cushioned and doesn’t swirl when you spin. Blood isn’t the cause either. The sensation comes from a few drops of fluid in three canals each about 0.3 mm across, plus the brain’s processing of their signal. Toggle the myth: the brain stays put while the cupula does all the bending.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Torsion-pendulum canal → first-order high-pass. The classic Steinhausen model treats the canal as a heavily damped torsion pendulum. Over everyday frequencies, cupula deflection follows head angular velocity through a high-pass filter: dc/dt = dω/dt − c/τc, with τc = 6 s. Deflection is shown in “°/s equivalent”, the head speed it signals, not in micrometres. (The real cupula moves by only nanometres to micrometres.)
  2. Felt rotation (velocity storage). The brain extends the canal signal with a central integrator (Raphan, Matsuo & Cohen 1979). We model the felt rotation as one high-pass filter with τ = 16 s when your eyes are closed or it’s dark, a typical value inside the 15–20 s range. With your eyes open in a lit room, vision cuts velocity storage short, so the vestibular term decays with about the cupula’s own 6 s. Vision is also blended in (60% weight) for the felt rotation. Real perception adds adaptation, so the after-spin is often a bit weaker than this simple model gives.
  3. Mismatch is the vestibular estimate minus the true head rotation, which is also what your eyes report in a lit room. It’s a stand-in for the sensory conflict thought to drive dizziness and motion sickness, not a measured quantity.
  4. Nystagmus. The eyes drift opposite to the vestibular signal (gain about 0.6 in the dark) and snap back in quick phases. When you fix your gaze on something with your eyes open, the drift is mostly suppressed, drawn here at 20%.
  5. Chair. Spin-up and stopping use a 0.15 s time constant, nearly a step. Only the horizontal (yaw) canal is modeled. Real canals come in three planes, and tilting your head while spinning stirs the others, which is why that makes you so much dizzier. Time constants are from Fernández & Goldberg (1971) and standard vestibular texts. Individual values vary.
  6. Safety. Spin seated, with space around you, and stop if you feel sick. Dizziness that comes without spinning, or that lasts, is worth seeing a doctor about.

The decaying step response is a nice real-world exponential. Misconceptions to name: “your brain sloshes”, “blood rushes to your head”, and “you feel rotation itself rather than changes in rotation”.
