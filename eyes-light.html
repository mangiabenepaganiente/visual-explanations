Why your eyes don’t send out rays

Many people, adults included, picture sight as something going out of the eyes. It’s the reverse. A light source sends light out in all directions. Some of it hits an object and scatters off its rough surface. A tiny fraction of that happens to head toward you and enters your pupil, and the lens focuses it onto the retina. Seeing is receiving. In a sealed, dark room nothing arrives, so you see nothing, however hard you look.

Try thisPress Light off. Notice the count of rays reaching your eye stops dead, and the retina image goes black, even though your eye is still open and aimed at the apple.

Cause 01

A source makes the light

Lamps, the Sun, and screens emit light. Everything else you see is only passing on light it received. Turn the lamp off in a sealed room and there is simply no light to pass on.

Cause 02

Objects scatter it everywhere

A rough, matte surface sends the light it doesn’t absorb off in all directions (diffuse reflection). That’s why you can see the apple from anywhere in the room. A white mug returns ~80% of the light, a red apple ~30%, and black velvet only ~3%.

Cause 03

Your pupil catches a sliver

Only rays aimed at a hole a few millimeters wide get in. The lens bends every ray from one point on the apple to one point on the retina, forming an upside-down image. Widening the pupil from 2 to 8 mm gives (8/2)² = 16× the area, which is why the pupil opens in the dark.

Myth, busted

“Eyes send out rays to see”

This idea (extramission) goes back to ancient Greece, and surveys find a sizable share of students, and even adults, still hold it. Toggle the myth: the red beams leave your eye. If that were how sight worked, you could see in a pitch-dark room. You can’t. The eye is a detector, and nothing in it sends anything out.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Real ray tracing, in 2D. Each frame, about 5,000 rays leave the point lamp in random directions, spread evenly. Directions that can reach the object are sampled more densely, and the counts are reweighted so “per million emitted” means per million rays spread evenly over all directions. They are intersected exactly with the object (a circle), the tabletop, the viewer’s head, the pupil opening, and the walls. At the object a ray survives with probability equal to the surface’s reflectance and leaves in a Lambertian (cosine-weighted) random direction: ideal diffuse reflection. Only rays that pass through the pupil opening from outside, within 60° of the eye’s axis, count as reaching the eye. Steeper rays are treated as blocked by brow and eyelids. The pupil is drawn enlarged, but hit-testing uses its true size.
  2. Honest simplifications. The room is a flat 2D slice, so rays spread over a circle, not a sphere, and the ray counts fall off as 1/r instead of 1/r². The walls and table are perfectly black (real rooms bounce a lot of light around, which is why shadows aren’t pitch black). Only one bounce is traced. Colors and wavelengths aren’t traced. Reflectances are rough typical values: white glazed mug ≈ 0.8, red apple ≈ 0.3 averaged over visible light, black velvet ≈ 0.03.
  3. 3D readouts. Illuminance on the object uses the real 3D inverse-square law, E ∝ 1/d², shown relative to a lamp 1 m away. Pupil area is π(d/2)². The relative image brightness on the retina is proportional to object luminance × pupil area, with luminance = reflectance × E / π. It does not depend on how far away you stand: the image gets smaller, but not dimmer.
  4. Eye optics. The retina image is found with the chief ray through the center of the lens (a simplified reduced eye, ~24 mm long), which is why it comes out inverted. Focusing adjustments (accommodation), the cornea’s share of the bending, and aberrations are ignored.
  5. Misconception research. Extramission beliefs have been documented in surveys of U.S. college students (Winer, Cottrell and colleagues, American Psychologist, 2002) and in physics-education studies of how students explain vision. Reported rates vary widely with how the question is asked.
  6. For teachers. Waves and electromagnetic radiation: an object is seen when light reflected from its surface enters the eye (NGSS 4-PS4-2, MS-PS4-2). Good follow-ups: the dark-box demo (a sealed shoebox with an object inside and a peephole), and why cats’ eyes “shine” (reflected lamp light, not light made by the eye).
