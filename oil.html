Oil floats because of density — not because it’s “lighter”

People say oil is lighter than water. That mixes up weight of a blob with density. Density is mass per volume: ρ = m / V. For the same volume, vegetable oil (~0.91 g/cm³) has less mass than water (1.00 g/cm³) — so it floats. Pour more oil and ρ stays the same; a huge oil spill still floats.

Cause 01

ρ = m / V

Density is mass packed into a volume. Water at room temperature is about 1.00 g/cm³. Typical vegetable oil is about 0.91 g/cm³. Compare equal volumes and oil simply has less mass.

Cause 02

Layers sort by density

Immiscible liquids settle so the denser one sits below. Oil floats on water because ρoil < ρwater. Drag the slider past 1.00 and the cartoon jar flips — the rule did not change.

Cause 03

More oil ≠ denser oil

Pouring twice as much oil doubles mass and volume. The ratio ρ stays put. A thimble of oil and a tanker of oil both float for the same reason.

Myth, busted

Not “because it’s lighter”

“Heavy things sink” confuses weight with density. A steel ship can float; a tiny pebble sinks. Ask: mass per volume? Oil’s equal-V mass is smaller — that is the density story, not a vague “lightness.”

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Density definition. ρ = m / V. For a chosen volume V, mass is m = ρ V. The equal-volume panel uses the same V_ref for oil and water so the mass labels are a direct density comparison. Units here are g/cm³ (identical numerically to g/mL); multiply by 1000 for kg/m³.
  2. Reference values. Liquid water is taken as ρ_water = 1.00 g/cm³ — the usual classroom round number near 20 °C (CIPM-2001 gives ≈0.998 at 20 °C; the 1% gap is ignored). Default oil is ρ_oil = 0.91 g/cm³, a typical vegetable-oil handbook range (~0.91–0.93). Olive, canola, and sunflower sit in that band; exact brand values vary.
  3. Layer rule. For two immiscible liquids that have finished separating, the denser fluid occupies the lower layer. The jar is a cartoon: no emulsifiers, no stirring, no temperature gradients. When ρ_oil > ρ_water the oil layer is drawn below — a counterfactual for most kitchen oils, but real for some dense syrups / honey vs water.
  4. “More oil” control. The volume slider scales oil volume as V_oil = k · V_ref with k from 0.5 to 3. Density is held fixed, so m_oil = ρ_oil · V_oil scales with k while ρ_oil does not. That is the point of the control: amount is not density.
  5. Buoyancy (Archimedes). A blob floats when average density is less than the surrounding fluid. For an oil droplet fully surrounded by water, buoyancy exceeds weight when ρ_oil < ρ_water. The page teaches the density comparison; it does not integrate surface tension or droplet shape.
  6. Miscibility. Real oil–water systems can emulsify with soap or vigorous mixing. The explainer assumes clean, settled, immiscible layers — the salad-dressing end state after sitting, not a shaken vinaigrette.
  7. Honey chip. Honey is often ~1.4 g/cm³; the slider caps at 1.30 so the chip clamps to the max. It is a density-invert demo, not a culinary model of honey–water mixing (honey is water-soluble).
  8. For teachers. Density vs “heavy/light” language; equal-volume thought experiment; NGSS MS-PS1 structure and properties of matter. Pairs with the ice-floats page (lattice density) without repeating it. Say out loud: “lighter” without a volume is ambiguous — density is the precise claim.
