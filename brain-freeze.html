Why brain freeze isn’t in your brain

That stabbing forehead ache after a fast spoonful of ice cream has a medical name: sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. Your brain doesn’t get cold. It stays at about 37 °C the whole time. The cold hits the roof of your mouth, where blood vessels first squeeze shut and then rush open to rewarm the tissue. In a small 2012 study, blood flow in the anterior cerebral artery tended to rise during brain freeze. The trigeminal nerve carries the signal, and your brain blames your forehead. That’s called referred pain.

Try thisPress Take a bite and wait. Notice the pain doesn’t start when the cold touches your palate. It arrives about 10 seconds later, when the vessels swing from squeezing to widening.

Cause 01

Cold hits the roof of your mouth

Ice cream around −12 °C pressed against the palate pulls its surface temperature down fast. The palate is thin, packed with blood vessels and nerve endings, and sits just below a nerve hub behind your nose, the sphenopalatine ganglion. Cold drinks gulped against the palate do the same.

Cause 02

Squeeze, then flood

Vessels first constrict to save heat, then dilate hard to warm the area back up. In a small 2012 study from Jorge Serrador’s lab (13 adults), flow in the anterior cerebral artery tended to rise during the pain and fall as it eased. The rapid widening is thought to set off the pain, but the evidence is still preliminary.

Cause 03

The nerve blames your forehead

Pain from the palate and these vessels travels on branches of the trigeminal nerve, the same nerve that serves your forehead and face. The brain can’t tell which branch the signal came from, so it reports pain in the forehead or behind the eyes. That’s referred pain, the same trick behind a heart attack felt in the arm.

Myth, busted

“Your brain is freezing”

Your brain is wrapped in skull and fluid and fed about 750 mL of 37 °C blood every minute. A spoonful of ice cream can’t chill it measurably. Toggle the myth: the brain thermometer never moves while the pain comes and goes. Brain freeze is a signal, not a temperature.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. This model is illustrative, not a physiological simulation. The shapes of the curves follow the published story: cold contact, brief vasoconstriction, then a delayed dilation that tracks the pain. The time constants are tuned so a 5 s bite of −12 °C ice cream gives pain starting about 8 s in and lasting about 20 s. Actual timing varies a lot between people. Many people never get brain freeze at all.
  2. Palate temperature. First-order heat balance: dT/dt = (36 − T)/τblood + contact·(Tfood − T)/τfood + tongue·(35 − T)/τtongue, with τ = 15 s, 6 s and 3 s. The food is treated as staying at its starting temperature during contact, which overstates cooling for long contact.
  3. Vessels and pain. A cold stimulus s = (29 °C − T)/10 °C (0 when warmer) drives a fast transient constriction (high-pass, τ 2.5 s) and a delayed dilation (two 6 s low-pass stages). Pain rises once dilation passes a threshold. “Vessel width” is relative to resting and only illustrative. It is not a measured artery diameter. The 2012 study measured blood flow velocity, not diameter.
  4. Sources. Blatt M., Falvo M., Jasien J., Deegan B., Ó Laighin G. & Serrador J., “Cerebral Vascular Blood Flow Changes During ‘Brain Freeze’”, FASEB J 26(S1):685.4 (2012), a conference abstract. Thirteen adults sipped ice water against the palate while transcranial Doppler tracked flow. Anterior cerebral artery flow velocity tended to rise (P = 0.078, a trend, not a firm result). Hulihan J., “Ice cream headache”, BMJ 1997;314:1364. Kaczorowski M. & Kaczorowski J., “Ice cream evoked headaches (ICE-H) study”, BMJ 2002;325:1445, where eating fast roughly doubled the rate in students. The medical term is used in the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) as “headache attributed to ingestion or inhalation of a cold stimulus”.
  5. The tongue trick works in the model the same way it is thought to in life. It rewarms the palate quickly, so the cold stimulus ends sooner and the dilation fades. Warm water does the same. Eating slowly keeps the palate from cooling much in the first place.
  6. Brain temperature is shown as a constant 37 °C. The brain receives roughly 15% of cardiac output (about 750 mL/min) and is well insulated, so the heat absorbed by one bite is carried off by blood in the mouth and face long before it could reach the brain.
  7. For teachers. Covers the nervous system, sensory receptors, feedback in body systems, and heat transfer (NGSS MS-LS1-3, MS-LS1-8, HS-LS1-2). Misconceptions to name: “your brain freezes”, and “pain is felt where it happens” (referred pain).
