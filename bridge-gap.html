Why bridges need gaps

Steel and concrete grow when they warm up and shrink when they cool. The change is small per meter, but bridges are long: ΔL = α·L·ΔT, so a 100 m steel span gets about 6 cm longer from a winter night to a summer afternoon. Those toothed metal plates you drive over are expansion joints, the room the deck needs to move. Take them away and the deck pushes on its supports with σ = E·α·ΔT, about 120 MPa for steel. That’s enough to buckle it.

Cause 01

Warm atoms take up more room

Heating makes atoms vibrate harder and sit slightly farther apart, so a whole beam grows in proportion to its length: ΔL = α·L·ΔT. For steel α ≈ 12×10⁻⁶ /°C, just 0.012 mm per meter per degree. Over 100 m and 50 °C that adds up to 60 mm.

Cause 02

Blocked growth becomes force

If the ends can’t move, the deck is squeezed back by exactly the strain it wanted: σ = E·α·ΔT. For steel that’s 2.4 MPa per degree, independent of length. A 50 °C rise means 120 MPa pushing on the abutments, and a long, slender deck buckles well before the steel itself fails.

Cause 03

Joints and bearings let it breathe

One end of a span is usually pinned. The other sits on sliding or rocking bearings and meets the next span at an expansion joint: interlocking steel fingers or a modular strip seal. The gap opens in winter and closes in summer, and traffic rolls across.

Myth, busted

“Bridges are rigid” · “those gaps are sloppy building”

Nothing that size is rigid against temperature. The gap is engineered, sized from the local temperature range, the material and the span. Leaving it out is the mistake: locked-in decks, and rails without enough allowance, buckle in heat waves. Toggle the myth to see the rigid-bridge prediction.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Free expansion. ΔL = α·L·(T − T0), linear in temperature, with the deck built (joint set) at T0 = 15 °C. Coefficients: structural steel 12×10⁻⁶ /°C, concrete 10×10⁻⁶ /°C (typical range 10–12×10⁻⁶, depending on the aggregate), aluminum alloy 23×10⁻⁶ /°C. “Deck temperature” is the effective average temperature of the deck. In sun a deck runs hotter than the air, and on clear nights colder.
  2. Restrained stress. If both ends are fixed, σ = E·α·ΔT (uniaxial, fully restrained, uniform temperature). E: steel 200 GPa, concrete 30 GPa, aluminum 69 GPa. Hot gives compression, cold gives tension. The force on the supports is σ times the deck’s cross-section, so we report it per square meter of section.
  3. Verdict thresholds (indicative, not a design check): steel yields at 250 MPa (common structural grade) and aluminum at 240 MPa (6061-T6). The “buckling risk” lines (80 MPa steel, 60 MPa aluminum) are illustrative. Real buckling depends on slenderness, restraint and imperfections. For scale, continuous welded rail is prone to heat kinks roughly 30–40 °C above its stress-free temperature, about 70–100 MPa. Concrete: we use 45% of a 30 MPa compressive strength (13.5 MPa) as a crushing and spalling warning, and about 3 MPa tension for cracking.
  4. Joint sizing. The joint is set at 15 °C to accommodate an illustrative design range of −20 to +50 °C plus 10 mm minimum clearance: gap = α·L·(50 − 15) + 10 mm − ΔL. Real design ranges come from local climate data and codes, such as AASHTO LRFD in the U.S. and Eurocode EN 1991-1-5. Above the design range the joint closes and the deck becomes restrained from that point on. That’s the edge case the heat-wave chip shows.
  5. Left out. Bearing friction, temperature gradients through the deck depth (which make decks curl), creep and shrinkage of concrete, and the fact that multi-span bridges split the movement among several joints. The drawing exaggerates the movement so you can see it. The joint inset shows the true gap in millimeters.
  6. Not the same as thermal shock. A cracking glass (see the hot-water explainer) fails because one part heats faster than another. A bridge has a different problem: the whole deck heats evenly but its ends are held in place.
  7. For teachers. Thermal energy and particle motion (MS-PS1-4, MS-PS3), engineering design under constraints (MS-ETS1, HS-ETS1). Lab idea: measure a long aluminum rod’s growth in hot water with a dial gauge, then scale it up to your local bridge.
