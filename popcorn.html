Why popcorn pops

It is not air bursting out. A popcorn kernel holds about 14% water sealed inside a hard, nearly airtight hull (the pericarp). Heat turns that water into steam, and the pressure climbs with temperature until, near 180 °C and roughly 9 atm, the hull fails. The hot, softened starch flashes into foam and sets as the fluffy flake.

Try thisPress Heat pan, then drag moisture down to 9% and heat again. Notice the pressure curve never reaches the hull line — dry kernels just sit there.

Cause 01

Water becomes steam

The starchy endosperm holds roughly 13–14% water. Heated in a sealed hull, that water cannot boil off at 100 °C; its vapor pressure keeps climbing — about 10 atm for pure water at 180 °C (steam tables).

Cause 02

The hull is a pressure vessel

The pericarp is tough and low-permeability. It holds until the internal pressure passes its strength — reported around ~0.9 MPa ≈ 9 atm near 180 °C. Then it fails suddenly: the “pop.”

Cause 03

Starch flashes into foam

By then the starch is hot, wet, and gelatinized. When the pressure drops to 1 atm, the steam expands it into a foam that cools and sets in a fraction of a second — the white flake is 20–40× the kernel’s volume.

Myth, busted

“The air inside expands”

Air trapped at 1 atm and heated from 20 to 180 °C at fixed volume only reaches 453/293 ≈ 1.5 atm — far short of ~9 atm. Toggle the myth: an air-only kernel never pops. It’s the water. Dry kernels (below ~10%) and cracked hulls fail.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Steam pressure. Saturation vapor pressure of water from the Antoine equation, log₁₀ p[mmHg] = A − B/(C + T), with A = 8.07131, B = 1730.63, C = 233.426 below 99 °C and A = 8.14019, B = 1810.94, C = 244.485 above (standard fitted constants). It matches IAPWS steam tables to within about 1% from 25 to 220 °C (e.g. 1.00 MPa at 180 °C).
  2. Bound water (the big simplification). Water held in starch has a lower vapor pressure than free water. The page scales steam pressure by a toy water-activity curve aw = 1 / (1 + (11.5 / M)6), M in % moisture. That gives aw ≈ 0.77 at 14%, which is roughly the humidity that grain at 14% moisture comes to equilibrium with at room temperature. The curve is a teaching fit, not a measured isotherm, and it ignores how aw changes with temperature. It is what makes dry kernels fail in this model.
  3. Trapped air. Pore air starts at 1 atm and is treated as an ideal gas at fixed volume: p = 1 atm × T/T₀ (kelvin). Internal pressure = air + aw · psat(T), relaxing toward that value with a 1 s lag.
  4. Hull strength. The nominal failure pressure is 0.93 MPa (≈ 9.2 atm), with ±8% kernel-to-kernel scatter. Published estimates cluster near 0.9–1.0 MPa at about 180 °C (Hoseney et al., Cereal Chem. 1983; Virot & Ponomarenko, J. R. Soc. Interface 12, 2015, which measured a critical popping temperature near 180 °C). Treat these numbers as approximate.
  5. Leaks and moisture loss. About 5% of each batch has micro-cracked hulls. Cracked hulls vent steam (leak rate 4 s⁻¹ against a 1 s build-up lag), so the excess pressure stalls at a fifth of the sealed value or less. Intact hulls also lose a little water as the pressure rises (0.01 percentage points per second per MPa), so very slow heating costs a few pops. Both rates are illustrative, not measured.
  6. Temperature. Kernel temperature ramps at the chosen rate from 20 °C and is capped at 220 °C (hot oil or air-popper range). Heat conduction inside the kernel, starch chemistry, and burning are not modeled. Flake size is decorative.
  7. For teachers. This covers phase change, vapor pressure vs temperature, and pressure vessels (NGSS MS-PS1-4 and HS-PS3). Try a lab: weigh kernels before and after oven-drying them to measure moisture, then compare pop yields. Say the misconception out loud: “the air inside expands.”
