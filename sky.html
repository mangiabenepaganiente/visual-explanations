Why the sky is blue — and sunsets red

Air is not blue stuff, and the sky does not borrow the ocean. Molecules scatter sunlight as λ−4. Looking up you see the scattered short wavelengths. Looking at the Sun through a long air mass you see the leftover: orange, then red.

Cause 01

Molecules scatter as λ⁻⁴

N₂ and O₂ are far smaller than visible wavelengths, so Rayleigh scattering goes as 1/λ⁴. Light at 450 nm is tossed sideways (650/450)⁴ ≈ 4.36× more than light at 650 nm.

Cause 02

Geometry: scatter vs leftover

Looking up, you see the light that was scattered — short-λ wins. Looking at the Sun through a long air mass, you see what remains after that short-λ has been stripped out.

The path

Air mass grows at low Sun

Overhead, X = 1. Near the horizon, Kasten–Young air mass climbs past 10, then ~38. Beam transmittance e^{−τX} kills blue first. Zenith stays bluish until twilight.

Myth, busted

It is not the ocean

The sky is blue over wheat fields and deserts. Air is not a blue gas. Oceans look blue partly because they see the sky, and because water absorbs red — the arrow goes the other way.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Rayleigh scattering. For particles much smaller than the wavelength, the scattering cross-section σ ∝ 1/λ⁴ across the visible. Relative scatter σ(λ₁)/σ(λ₂) = (λ₂/λ₁)⁴. At the classroom pair: (650/450)⁴ ≈ 4.36. This page does not treat air as a blue pigment.
  2. Air mass. Zenith angle z = 90° − elev. For z < 70° we use the plane-parallel X = 1/cos z = 1/sin(elev), which is exactly 1 at the zenith. For z ≥ 70° we switch to Kasten & Young 1989: X = 1 / [sin γ + 0.50572 (γ + 6.07995)^{−1.6364}] with γ in degrees. That formula stays finite at the horizon (X ≈ 37.9). A raw 1/sin(elev) would blow up.
  3. Optical depth and the leftover beam. Vertical Rayleigh optical depth τ(λ) = 0.12 · (550 nm / λ)⁴ — a typical clear sea-level column, no aerosols. Direct-beam transmittance T = exp(−τ X). Because τ is larger in the blue, a large air mass strips short-λ first and the transmitted Sun goes yellow → orange → red.
  4. Looking up (zenith still blue). Single-scattering sketch: I(λ) ∝ F(λ) · σ(λ) · exp(−τ X_in) · (1 − exp(−τ X_view)). Overhead scatterers sit high, so incoming air mass is only a fraction of the solar air mass: X_in = 1 + (0.18 + 0.55 t)(X − 1), t = 1 − viewElev/90. That is why the zenith stays bluish until twilight even while the Sun on the horizon is red. A pinch of wavelength-flat Mie / multiple-scatter is mixed in near the horizon so noon’s horizon is milky, not neon.
  5. Solar spectrum. Three channels at 650 / 550 / 450 nm, relative Planck function at 5772 K (hc/k = 1.438777×10⁷ nm·K), normalized to 550 nm. Display uses Reinhard tonemapping and γ = 2.2. This is not a CIE XYZ transform and not a photograph.
  6. Clouds are a different scatter. Cloud droplets are ~10 μm, much larger than λ, so Mie scattering is almost color-blind — clouds look white or gray. Same sunlight, different particle size. Fog and haze also pull the sky toward white.
  7. Ozone is not why the sky is blue. The Chappuis bands (roughly 500–650 nm) are a sunset nuance — they can nudge a red Sun toward orange — not the primary-blue mechanism. We do not invent ozone-as-primary-blue.
  8. Drawings are not maps. No polarization, no multiple-scattering radiative transfer, no green flash, no refraction of the solar disk. The Sun’s drawn size is schematic. Real horizons include aerosols that this clear-Rayleigh column omits.
