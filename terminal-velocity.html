Why a skydiver stops speeding up

Step out of a plane and gravity pulls you down with your full weight, m·g, the whole way. Air pushes back with drag ½·ρ·CdA·v², which grows fast as you speed up. After about 12 seconds, drag has grown to equal your weight. The net force is then zero and you stop accelerating: terminal velocity, about 54 m/s (120 mph) belly-down. Gravity hasn’t gone anywhere.

Cause 01

Gravity pulls with m·g, always

Your weight doesn’t change as you fall. An 80 kg skydiver is pulled down with 785 N at the door of the plane, at 120 mph, and under the canopy. At the start there’s no drag yet, so you gain 9.8 m/s every second.

Cause 02

Drag grows with speed squared

Air resistance is F = ½·ρ·CdA·v². Twice as fast means four times the drag. As speed builds, drag catches up with weight and the net force, and so the acceleration, shrinks toward zero.

Cause 03

Terminal: drag = weight

Setting drag equal to weight gives vt = √(2mg / ρCdA). Tuck into head-down (small CdA) and it rises to ~85 m/s. Open a canopy (huge CdA) and it drops to ~5 m/s. Thinner air up high means faster falls there.

Myth, busted

“You keep speeding up” / “gravity stops”

Both are wrong. Without air you would speed up forever (the red line in myth mode). With air you don’t, but not because gravity quits. At terminal velocity gravity is fully on and balanced by an equal drag force. Zero net force means constant speed (Newton’s first law), not zero speed.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Equation of motion. Vertical only: m·dv/dt = m·g − ½·ρ(h)·CdA·v², with g = 9.80665 m/s² held constant (it is only ~0.3% weaker at 10 km). Integrated with 4th-order Runge–Kutta at 5 ms steps. At constant ρ this reproduces the exact solution v = vt·tanh(g·t/vt), and the validator checks that. The plane’s forward speed at exit and any horizontal drift are ignored.
  2. Air density from the International Standard Atmosphere: T = 288.15 − 0.0065·h K, p = 101325·(T/288.15)5.2559 Pa up to 11 km, isothermal 216.65 K above, and ρ = p/(287.05·T). That gives 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level and 0.819 at 4 km. Real days differ by a few percent. The ground is taken to be at sea level.
  3. Drag areas CdA are effective values chosen to match typical published speeds for an 80 kg jumper: belly-to-earth 0.44 m² (≈ 54 m/s at sea level, ≈ 120 mph), sit-fly 0.30 m² (≈ 65 m/s), head-down 0.18 m² (≈ 84 m/s). Real values vary with body size, jumpsuit, and arch. The page holds CdA fixed when you change mass, so vt ∝ √m. Real heavier people are also larger, which weakens that effect.
  4. Parachute. A simplified deployment: 1 s of pilot chute and line stretch (CdA ≈ 1.5 m²), then 2.5 s of inflation with CdA rising smoothly to an effective 42 m², which gives ≈ 5.5 m/s steady descent for 80 kg at sea level. Peak opening deceleration comes out around 3–5 g, in the range jumpers report for normal openings. Real ram-air canopies also glide forward at ~10 m/s. Only the vertical rate is modeled. If you don’t open it, the canopy opens automatically at 900 m, a typical deployment altitude.
  5. Time display. Freefall plays at ×2 speed and canopy flight at ×10 so a whole jump fits in about a minute. Clocks and readouts show real elapsed time.
  6. For teachers. Newton’s first and second laws with a velocity-dependent force (NGSS HS-PS2-1). Common misconceptions are that heavier objects keep accelerating, that a constant speed needs a net force, or that gravity stops at terminal velocity. Heavier jumpers do reach a higher vt (√m), which is why skydivers wear weight belts to match speeds with lighter friends.
