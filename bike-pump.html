Why a bike pump gets warm

Rubbing plays a small part. The big effect is that pushing the piston does work on the air. That energy goes into the air molecules, which then move faster — the air gets hotter. For a quick push with no time to lose heat, T₂ = T₁·(P₂/P₁)(γ−1)/γ. Squeezing room air to 7 atm could, in the ideal case, heat it past 200 °C. The hot air sits at the valve end, which is why the bottom of the pump warms first.

Try thisPress +20 strokes, then look at the barrel thermometer. Notice the valve end is far warmer than the top, even though the piston seal rubs along the whole barrel.

Cause 01

Work in → energy in the air

The piston pushes molecules that bounce off it, and they rebound faster, like a ball off an advancing bat. By the first law, ΔU = W − Q. The work you do (W = −∫P dV) raises the air’s internal energy, which for a gas means its temperature.

Cause 02

Fast = hot, slow = cool

Push quickly and the heat has no time to leak away (adiabatic): T₂ = T₁(P₂/P₁)0.286 for air (γ = 1.4). Push slowly and the barrel soaks it up, keeping the air near room temperature (isothermal). Real strokes fall in between. The P–V diagram shows exactly where.

Cause 03

The valve end gets it

The air is hottest at the end of the stroke, squeezed into the bottom of the barrel beside the valve and hose. That is where the metal picks up most of the heat. After a few dozen strokes, the base of the pump is warm to the touch.

Myth, busted

“It’s just the piston rubbing”

Seal friction is real, but it spreads along the whole length the piston travels. It predicts a gently, evenly warm barrel. The measured pattern is a hot bottom and a cool top, which only compression explains. Toggle the myth to see how badly friction alone under-predicts the valve end. The same heating happens in diesel engines, where compression alone ignites the fuel.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Air as an ideal diatomic gas. PV = nRT, cv = R/(γ−1), γ = 1.4, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Ideal limits: adiabatic T₂ = T₁(P₂/P₁)(γ−1)/γ (1 → 7 atm from 293 K gives 511 K) with work (P₂V₂ − P₁V₁)/(γ−1). Isothermal work is nRT·ln(V₁/V₂). Both are checked in validate-bike-pump.js.
  2. One stroke, integrated in time. Piston motion x(t) = S(1 − cos πt/ts)/2. The barrel (32 mm bore, 500 mm stroke, 15 mL dead volume in the hose and valve) starts each stroke full of room air at 1 atm. With the valve shut: n·cv·dT/dt = −P·dV/dt − h·Awet·(T − Twall). Once P reaches tire pressure, the check valve opens and air flows out at constant P (open-system energy balance: n·cp·dT/dt = −h·A·(T − Twall)). The tire is treated as a large reservoir already at the target pressure, like the last, hardest strokes. The first-law balance W = ΔU + Hout + Q closes to <0.5%.
  3. Heat-loss parameter. A single convective coefficient h (default 40 W/m²·K, an order-of-magnitude guess) between air and barrel sets how adiabatic a stroke is. The ratio of the air’s thermal time constant to the push time matters. The readout “polytropic n” (PVn fitted up to valve opening) shows it: 1.0 = isothermal, 1.4 = adiabatic. Set h = 0 for the ideal adiabatic case.
  4. Barrel thermometer. The aluminium barrel (250 g, c = 900 J/kg·K) is split into 8 segments. Air-to-wall heat lands only on the segments below the piston (plus the base). Conduction along the tube (k = 200 W/m·K, 1.5 mm wall) and slow cooling to 20 °C room air (h = 10 W/m²·K) smooth it. The metal is heavy compared with ~0.5 g of air per stroke, which is why the barrel warms by degrees, not hundreds of degrees. The air itself is briefly much hotter.
  5. Friction estimate. The cup seal drags with F = 8 N + 0.15 × (P − Patm) × contact band (π·D·3 mm). The pressure term is included on purpose: a pressurized seal presses harder, so friction is also somewhat larger near the bottom. The friction heat ∫F|dx| (down and up strokes) goes into the segment at the seal. “Friction only” runs a second barrel with only that heat, which is the myth’s prediction.
  6. What’s left out. Real pumps rarely finish full strokes at high pressure. Air also heats the hose, valve and tire, and leaks past the seal. Refill air is taken at room temperature (re-expansion of the dead volume is ignored). The tire’s own pressure rise stroke by stroke is not modeled. Real pump air stays far below the ideal 511 K because heat flows to the barrel and strokes are partial. The trend (fast strokes run hotter, the valve end heats most, friction is the smaller share) is robust. Individual degrees are not.
  7. For teachers. Energy transfer and the first law (NGSS HS-PS3-1, HS-PS3-2), plus kinetic theory (MS-PS1-4). Classic companions are the fire syringe (fire piston) and a diesel engine’s compression ignition. Name the misconception: “the pump gets hot from friction.”
