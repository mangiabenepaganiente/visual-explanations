Why magnets don’t stick to all metals

A fridge magnet ignores a soda can, a copper pipe and a gold ring. Being metal, or shiny, isn’t the test. Every atom’s electrons act as tiny magnets, but only in ferromagnets (iron, nickel, cobalt, most steels) do neighboring atoms lock together into domains that a magnet can swing into line. Everything else responds about 100,000 times more weakly. Heat iron past its Curie temperature (770 °C) and its domains fall apart. The pull collapses about a hundredfold, and a fridge magnet lets go.

Cause 01

Every atom is a tiny magnet, or none

Electrons carry spin, and spinning charge makes a magnetic moment. In most atoms the electrons pair up and cancel. Iron, nickel and cobalt keep several unpaired spins, so each atom is a small bar magnet. Copper, gold and silver have none left over.

Cause 02

Domains make the difference

In a ferromagnet a quantum effect (the exchange interaction) locks billions of neighboring moments parallel, forming domains. The domains point every which way, so an unmagnetized nail looks non-magnetic. A magnet nearby swings them into line and the nail becomes a magnet that is pulled in. That’s a susceptibility in the thousands, not ~10⁻⁵.

Cause 03

Heat scrambles the lock

Thermal jiggling fights the exchange lock. At the Curie temperature it wins: domains vanish and spontaneous magnetization drops to zero. Iron: 1043 K (770 °C). Nickel: 627 K (354 °C). Cobalt: 1388 K (1115 °C). Above it the metal is only weakly paramagnetic and the pull collapses.

Myth, busted

“Magnets attract all metals”

Aluminum (χ ≈ +2.2×10⁻⁵) is attracted, but so faintly that a strong magnet pulls less than 1% of its weight. Copper, gold and silver (χ < 0) are pushed away just as faintly. A “silver” fridge door made of 304 stainless won’t hold a magnet. A 430 stainless door will. The test is the crystal’s spins, not shine or conductivity.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Magnet. An N42-grade neodymium disc, 20 mm across and 10 mm thick, remanence Br = 1.32 T. On its axis the field is the standard uniformly-magnetized-cylinder result, B(z) = (Br/2)·[(z+L)/√((z+L)²+R²) − z/√(z²+R²)], about 0.46 T at the face. Its gradient is taken analytically. The “fridge (ceramic) magnet” option uses the same shape with ferrite’s Br = 0.39 T. Real flexible fridge magnets are thinner, multi-pole strips and weaker still.
  2. Sample and force. Every sample is the same 6 mm sphere, centered 3 mm behind the gap. Force on a small magnetized body: F = V·M·dB/dz, with the field treated as uniform across the sphere (a point-dipole estimate, fair to a factor of about 2 at the smallest gaps). Positive means pulled in; negative means pushed away. “Pull ÷ weight” compares with the sphere’s own weight, ρVg. Above 1, it jumps to the magnet.
  3. Weak materials. Room-temperature SI volume susceptibilities from standard tables (CRC Handbook values): aluminum +2.2×10⁻⁵, copper −9.63×10⁻⁶, gold −3.44×10⁻⁵, silver −2.38×10⁻⁵, water −9.05×10⁻⁶. Pure brass is diamagnetic (≈ −1×10⁻⁵ used here), but workshop brass often has iron contamination. Annealed 304 stainless is austenitic and paramagnetic, μr ≈ 1.003–1.01 (χ = 5×10⁻³ used). Cold-working (bent edges, sink rims) converts some of it to magnetic martensite, so real 304 can tug noticeably at corners. These χ values are held constant with temperature, and water is drawn as liquid at every temperature, even though it would boil.
  4. Ferromagnets. μr is an order-of-magnitude “effective” value (iron 5000, low-carbon steel 1000, 430 stainless 750, nickel 600, cobalt 250). Real permeability depends strongly on field, purity and history (hysteresis is not modeled). Shape matters more: a sphere’s own demagnetizing field (factor N = 1/3) caps its effective susceptibility at χ/(1+Nχ) ≤ 3, which is why the iron, steel and cobalt bars look alike. Magnetization is also capped at saturation Ms(T): iron 1.71, steel 1.6, cobalt 1.42, 430 stainless 1.2, nickel 0.485 MA/m at room temperature. Nickel saturates first at small gaps.
  5. Temperature. Ms(T) comes from the Weiss mean-field model with spin ½: m = tanh((Tc·m + h)/T), where h = μBB/kB is the magnet’s field in kelvin. With h = 0 this gives exactly zero spontaneous magnetization at and above Tc. With the field on, a small induced (Curie–Weiss-like) magnetization remains, so the pull falls by about 99% within a few tens of degrees above Tc rather than instantly. That leftover pull is real: a strong neodymium magnet held very close can still carry a small piece of iron just past 770 °C, while a ceramic magnet drops it. Classroom Curie-point demos work because the pull falls below the sample’s weight or a spring’s tension. Mean field is a textbook approximation: real iron follows (1 − T/Tc)β with β ≈ 0.35 near Tc (dashed curve for comparison). Curie temperatures: Fe 1043 K, Ni 627 K, Co 1388 K. Low-carbon steel ≈ 1040 K; 430 stainless ≈ 940 K (chromium lowers it, and published values vary). Not modeled: iron’s change to non-magnetic γ-iron at 912 °C, cobalt’s 422 °C phase change, the Hopkinson permeability peak just below Tc, and oxidation.
  6. Domains picture. The arrows are a cartoon: real domains are ~1–100 µm across and grow by domain-wall motion as well as rotation. The displayed “domain alignment” is the net magnetization divided by the spontaneous magnetization, M/Ms(T). That is how much of the available order the magnet has lined up.
  7. For teachers. Fits magnetic forces and fields (NGSS MS-PS2-3, MS-PS2-5, HS-PS2-5) and structure–property links (HS-PS1-3). Easy checks: a US nickel (75% Cu) doesn’t stick, a UK or Canadian coin minted after the switch to plated steel does, and a strong magnet dropped down a copper pipe falls slowly. That last one is eddy currents from motion, a different effect than the static pull shown here. Say the misconception out loud: shiny or metal means magnetic.
