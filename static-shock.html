Why the doorknob zaps you

The knob isn’t charged, and metal doesn’t make electricity. You are the charged object. Each step on a carpet moves a little charge between your soles and the fibers (the triboelectric effect). Your body is a small capacitor, about 150 pF, so modest charge means big voltage: V = Q/C. In dry air the charge can’t leak away, and you reach kilovolts. Reach for metal and the air gap breaks down in a spark.

Cause 01

Rubbing moves charge

Different materials hold electrons with different strength. Each time a sole touches the carpet and peels away, a little charge moves across (the triboelectric effect). Which way it goes depends on the materials; here we draw it as extra electrons on you.

Cause 02

Small capacitor, big volts

Your body and the floor form a capacitor of roughly 100–200 pF (the standard human-body model uses 100 pF). With V = Q/C, just 1.5 µC gives 10 kV. The stored energy is only ½CV² ≈ 7.5 mJ, which stings but is harmless.

Cause 03

Dry air lets it build

Humid air leaves a thin film of water on skin, shoes, and carpet fibers that bleeds charge away. In dry winter air nothing drains it, so steps pile up charge. Typical walking-on-carpet peaks run from ~1.5 kV (humid) to ~35 kV (very dry).

Myth, busted

“The metal knob is charged”

The knob is neutral and generates nothing. It is simply a good conductor, connected to a big object. As your charged fingertip nears it, the field in the thin air gap passes air’s breakdown strength (~3 MV/m for centimeter gaps), the air ionizes, and your charge rushes out in a spark. A wooden door gives no spark: no easy path.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Body as a capacitor. C = 150 pF, the middle of the usual 100–200 pF range for a standing adult. The ESD human-body model (e.g. ANSI/ESDA/JEDEC JS-001) uses 100 pF behind 1.5 kΩ. Q = C·V and E = ½·C·V². Contact resistance, the discharge waveform, and the charge left on the knob side are not modeled.
  2. How high you get. Walking-on-carpet peaks are anchored to the widely reproduced ESD table: about 35 kV at 10–20% RH and about 1.5 kV at 65–90% RH. Between those, the page interpolates log-linearly (anchored at 15% and 75%) and caps at 35 kV. Footwear multiplies that peak by illustrative factors: rubber 1.0, leather 0.35, barefoot 0.05, ESD-safe 0.0025 (ESD footwear is meant to keep people under ~100 V). Real values vary a lot with carpet fiber, sole material, and gait.
  3. Charging per step. Each step moves you part of the way toward that peak: V(n) = Vpeak·(1 − e−n/8), which is 95% after about 24 steps. Walking tests such as ANSI/ESD STM97.2 show a similar climb over several seconds. The in-step flicker and the slow leakage while you stand still (seconds when humid, minutes when dry) are left out.
  4. Spark length. Uniform-field breakdown of air at 1 atm and 20 °C uses the empirical Schumann fit: V[kV] = 24.4·d + 6.53·√d, d in cm (30.9 kV at 1 cm, 4.5 kV at 1 mm). Paschen’s law puts the absolute minimum sparking voltage near 330 V, so below that there is no spark at any gap. A fingertip and a round knob make a non-uniform field, so real sparks can be somewhat longer. The simple 3 MV/m estimate is shown for comparison.
  5. Sensation. Rough rules of thumb from the ESD literature: you feel a discharge from about 2–3 kV (2.5 kV used here), hear a click from about 6 kV, and see the spark from about 8 kV. People vary. Holding a key moves the spark to the key tip, so the current enters your hand over a wide area and you don’t feel it.
  6. For teachers. This covers electric charge, fields, and energy (NGSS MS-PS2-3, MS-PS2-5, HS-PS2-4, HS-PS3-5). The discharge is real but low-energy (millijoules). Say the misconception out loud: metal makes or stores the shock.
