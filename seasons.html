Why Earth has seasons — tilt, not distance

Earth is closest to the Sun in January, not July. Seasons happen because a fixed 23.44° axial tilt aims one hemisphere into the sunlight, then the other — changing the Sun’s noon angle and the length of the day.

Cause 01

Angle / the flashlight rule

The same sunlight spread over a larger patch is weaker per square meter. Winter noon rays hit at a shallower angle, so the ground is dimmer even at midday.

Cause 02

Daylight hours

The tilted hemisphere spends more than 12 hours in the Sun. Longer days deposit more energy. At the Arctic Circle that becomes midnight sun — or polar night.

The Pivot

One fixed 23.5° tilt

The axis does not nod over the year. It points at Polaris in June and in December. Seasons change because that fixed lean faces the Sun, then away, then toward it again.

Myth, busted

It is not distance

Perihelion is ~January 3 — Northern winter. A 3.4% distance swing is real and small (~7% in flux) and it goes the wrong way for Northern seasons. Tilt is the whole story.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Declination (original one-term cosine). δ = −23.44° · cos(360° · (N + 10) / 365), N = day of year (Jan 1 = 1) on a 365-day non-leap calendar. Solstice extremes match. Equinox zero-crossings fall a day or two off the calendar chips: on Mar 20 (N = 79) and Sep 23 (N = 266), δ ≈ −0.9° instead of 0°. That is a cosmetic offset of this simple model, not a seasons error. A Fourier series (Spencer 1971) would hug the calendar equinoxes; we keep the original cosine.
  2. Sunrise equation. Hour angle of sunrise cos H₀ = −tan φ · tan δ. Daylight hours = 2 H₀ / 15° (i.e. 24 H₀ / π with H₀ in radians). Clamp: H₀ undefined when |tan φ tan δ| ≥ 1 → polar day (24 h) or polar night (0 h). No refraction, no solar-disk semi-diameter (the usual +0.833° correction is omitted), so the equator is exactly 12 h all year.
  3. Noon elevation. e = 90° − |φ − δ| on the noon meridian. Negative e means the Sun stays below the horizon (polar night). Light concentration relative to overhead is cos z = sin e when e > 0, else 0. This is the geometric / Lambertian factor only — no air mass or atmospheric extinction, which would dim low Sun further.
  4. Circular orbit + mean Sun. The drawn orbit is a circle. Real eccentricity is e ≈ 0.0167 (perihelion 147.1 million km ~Jan 3, aphelion 152.1 million km ~Jul 4). Distance is shown only as those two ticks; it does not drive the lighting. The date-to-angle mapping uses a mean Sun (constant angular speed), so Kepler’s equation is not applied. Perihelion and aphelion dates wander by about a day from year to year.
  5. Obliquity. Mean axial tilt 23.44° (IAU 2010 / current epoch, rounded). Arctic / Antarctic Circles at 90° − 23.44° = 66.56°; Tropics of Cancer / Capricorn at ±23.44°. The axis is drawn parallel to itself at every orbital post — it does not precess on this page (true precession period ≈ 25,772 years).
  6. Drawings are not maps. Sun and Earth are not to scale. The orbit view is a classroom top-down: the axis is shown in the plane of the page so the fixed lean is readable. The slice is the noon–midnight meridian with horizontal rays; the terminator is the vertical diameter. The flashlight beam is a schematic of equal photon count, not a rendered caustic.
