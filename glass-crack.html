Why hot water cracks a cold glass

Glass doesn’t mind being hot. It minds being unevenly hot. Glass conducts heat poorly, so boiling water heats the inner surface in a fraction of a second while the outer surface is still fridge-cold. The hot layer tries to expand, the cold layer holds it back, and the outer surface is stretched. Glass is weak in tension, so a scratch there can run into a crack.

Try thisPress Pour with the ice-cold soda-lime glass. Then switch to borosilicate and pour again. Same boiling water, same ΔT across the wall — about a third of the stress.

Cause 01

Glass is a poor conductor

Soda-lime glass conducts heat about 200× worse than aluminium (k ≈ 1 W/m·K). Heat soaks through a 3–4 mm wall over tens of seconds, but the inner surface jumps almost to the water temperature in under a second. For a moment the wall holds a steep temperature difference.

Cause 02

Hot side expands, cold side resists

Each layer wants to grow by α·ΔT. The layers are one solid piece, so the hot inner layer is squeezed (compression) and the cold outer layer is stretched (tension). A rough estimate of the stress is σ ≈ E·α·ΔT / (1 − ν).

Cause 03

Glass fails in tension at a flaw

Glass is very strong when squeezed but breaks when stretched at microscopic surface scratches. In practice that happens at around 30–70 MPa for annealed glass. The crack starts on the cold, stretched outer surface. Borosilicate expands only ~⅓ as much (α ≈ 3.3×10⁻⁶ /K), so the same ΔT makes about a third of the stress.

Myth, busted

“Boiling water is too hot for glass”

Glass is formed at ~1000 °C and softens around 550–600 °C. 100 °C is nothing to it. Warm the glass first and boiling water is usually fine. Pour ice water into a hot glass and it can crack with no hot water at all. What matters is the difference across the wall, not the water’s temperature.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Heat flow: 1D conduction through the wall. ∂T/∂t = (k/ρc)·∂²T/∂x² across the wall thickness, solved with an explicit finite-difference scheme (25 nodes, stable time step chosen automatically). Inner surface: convection to the water, h = 1500 W/m²·K (freshly poured, moving water; real values ~500–5000). Outer surface: still air at 22 °C, h = 10 W/m²·K. The water temperature is held fixed (a big pour relative to the glass). Curvature, the base, the rim and the waterline are not modeled.
  2. Stress: constrained thermal-shock estimate. The stress meter uses the standard engineering screen σ ≈ E·α·(Tin − Tout) / (1 − ν). It is biaxial (hoop + axial), with the colder surface in tension. This is a deliberately conservative value that treats the cold surface as fully held by the rest of the glass. A free, flat slab with the same instantaneous profile carries less: σ(x) = Eα(T̄ − T(x))/(1 − ν), shown as the “free-slab” sub-reading, typically 30–50% of the screen value. Real tumblers sit in between. Their thick bases, rims and the sharp hot/cold boundary at the waterline add constraint, and the waterline and base are where many real cracks start.
  3. Materials. Soda-lime: E = 70 GPa, α = 9.0×10⁻⁶ /K, ν = 0.22, k = 1.0 W/m·K, ρ = 2500 kg/m³, c = 840 J/kg·K. Borosilicate 3.3 (Pyrex/Duran-type): E = 64 GPa, α = 3.3×10⁻⁶ /K, ν = 0.20, k = 1.14, ρ = 2230, c = 830. Tempered soda-lime has the same heat and elastic properties, but its surface is pre-compressed by roughly 80–120 MPa, so tension has to cancel that first.
  4. Strength is statistical, not a single number. Glass breaks at surface flaws. Annealed glass in real use (scratched, washed, chipped) typically fails between ~30 and 70 MPa. The verdict uses below 30 MPa: survives, 30–45 MPa: at risk (a flawed glass may go), and 45 MPa and up: likely cracks. Tempered uses 100 / 150 MPa. A pristine lab rod can survive far more, and a deeply scratched tumbler less. Treat verdicts as “likely,” not certain.
  5. Where the crack starts. When hot water meets cold glass, the inner surface is in compression and the outer surface is in tension, so fracture typically starts on the outside and runs through the wall. When cold water meets hot glass, the signs flip and the wetted inner surface is stretched. That is often the more dangerous case, because the tensile surface is the one being shocked. The simple screen formula treats the two cases alike, and the free-slab sub-reading shows the difference.
  6. Thickness. Thicker walls take longer to even out (time ∝ L²) and hold a larger peak temperature difference. That is why thin lab glass and thin-walled tea glasses tolerate hot water better than thick tumblers and jars.
  7. For teachers. Thermal expansion, heat conduction and material properties (NGSS MS-PS1, HS-PS3). Safety: do not demonstrate cracking with real glassware near students. Use this page instead. Name the misconception: “boiling water is too hot for glass.”
