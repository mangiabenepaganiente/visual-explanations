Why your ears pop on a plane

An airliner keeps its cabin at the air pressure of a mountain town, about 6,000–8,000 ft, or roughly 75–81 kPa instead of 101 kPa at sea level. Behind each eardrum is a small pocket of air, the middle ear, which is sealed except for a thin Eustachian tube to your throat. When the cabin pressure changes and that pocket can’t keep up, the pressure difference bows the eardrum. The pop is the tube opening and the pressures evening out. It happens more easily on the way up than on the way down.

Cause 01

The cabin is a mountain town

Planes cruise around 35,000 ft, where air pressure is about 24 kPa. The cabin is pumped up, but not to sea level. U.S. rules cap the cabin at an equivalent 8,000 ft ≈ 75 kPa in normal flight, so pressure falls by about a quarter on the climb and rises back on the way down.

Cause 02

Middle-ear air is trapped

The middle ear is a sealed air pocket behind the eardrum. Its only vent is the Eustachian tube, which is normally closed. When cabin pressure changes, the trapped air keeps the old pressure and the difference pushes the drum. Even 1–2 kPa feels like fullness and muffled hearing.

Cause 03

Down is harder than up

Climbing, the trapped air is higher than the cabin, and it pushes the floppy tube open by itself, giving small automatic pops. Descending, the cabin is higher and squeezes the tube shut. Only swallowing or yawning pulls it open, and if you wait too long the difference can hold it closed.

Myth, busted

“Air is escaping from your head” · “that was your eardrum breaking”

The middle ear isn’t connected to your brain or sinuses behind it. Air moves only through the tube to your throat. The pop is the tube opening and the drum springing back to flat, not tearing. Rupture takes tens of kPa, far more than a normal flight. Toggle the myth to compare.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Cabin pressure comes from the International Standard Atmosphere: p = 101325·(1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵·h)5.25588 Pa with h in meters. 8,000 ft (2,438 m) gives 75.3 kPa; 6,000 ft gives 81.2 kPa. FAA rule 14 CFR 25.841 limits the cabin to 8,000 ft equivalent in normal operation. Many newer wide-bodies hold about 6,000 ft.
  2. Flight profile (illustrative): gate 0–5 min, climb 5–25 min, cruise 25–65 min, descent 65–87 min, landed at a sea-level airport. The cabin changes at roughly 300–400 ft/min, which is typical of airliner cabin climb and descent rates. Playback runs at 2 flight-minutes per second.
  3. Middle ear. Treated as a fixed volume of trapped air: its pressure changes only when the tube opens. Real eardrum stretch and slow gas absorption by the middle-ear lining are left out. Both are small over a flight.
  4. Tube behavior, approximate thresholds. Clear tube: when middle-ear pressure exceeds the cabin by about 1.5 kPa the tube is pushed open passively and vents until about 30% of that remains. A swallow equalizes fully if the difference is under about 12 kPa (≈ 90 mmHg). Above that, the negative pressure can hold the tube shut (“locking”). Head cold: 3 kPa passive vent, a swallow does half the job, locks above 6 kPa. Badly congested: 6 kPa, 15%, 2.5 kPa. These are rounded, order-of-magnitude values from the ENT and aviation-medicine literature. Real tubes vary a lot between people.
  5. How it feels (approximate bands for |Δp|): under 0.5 kPa nothing, up to 2 kPa fullness and muffled sound, up to 5 kPa discomfort, above that pain. Eardrum rupture is usually quoted at roughly 35 kPa (≈ 5 psi) or more, which a normal flight doesn’t reach.
  6. Not medical advice. If an ear stays blocked or painful after a flight, see a clinician. Babies cry on descent partly because sucking and swallowing open their tubes, so feeding during descent helps them.
