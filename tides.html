Why there are two high tides a day

The Moon does not just pull the water up. It stretches Earth into two bulges, and we rotate through both of them every lunar day — so a given place gets two high tides, about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.

Cause 01

Differential gravity

The near-side ocean feels a stronger lunar pull than Earth’s center does. That leftover acceleration is the near-side bulge — water drawn toward the Moon.

Cause 02

The far-side bulge

Earth’s center is pulled harder than the far water. Earth is tugged out from under that ocean, so a second bulge is left behind — away from the Moon.

Cause 03

We spin through both

A lunar day is ~24 h 50 min. Earth rotates through two bulges in that time, so highs arrive ~12 h 25 min apart. Watch the pin.

Myth, busted

The second tide is not the Sun

Leave the Sun off. Two highs remain. The Sun only changes how high (spring when aligned, neap at quadrature) — never how many.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Equilibrium tide. Tide-generating potential V = (GM R² / D³) P₂(cos θ) with P₂(x) = (3x² − 1)/2. Height ζ = V / g, g = 9.81 m/s². Scale GM_m R² / (g D³) ≈ 0.36 m at mean lunar distance; crest-to-trough ≈ 1.5× that ≈ 0.54 m (Moon only). This is a global ocean with no continents — the equilibrium tide, not a harbor prediction.
  2. Constants (SI). G = 6.67430×10⁻¹¹ (CODATA 2018); M_earth = 5.972168×10²⁴ kg, M_moon = 7.342×10²² kg, M_sun = 1.9885×10³⁰ kg; R_earth = 6.371×10⁶ m (mean volumetric); D_moon = 3.844×10⁸ m mean, perigee 363.3 Mm, apogee 405.5 Mm; D_sun = 1.495978707×10¹¹ m (IAU au). Sidereal day 86164.0905 s; sidereal month 27.321661 d. Lunar day T = 1 / (1/T_sid − 1/T_month) ≈ 24.841 h. Exact axis accelerations: a_near = GM/(D−R)², a_center = GM/D², a_far = GM/(D+R)²; residuals a_near − a_center and a_center − a_far.
  3. The drawn ocean is exaggerated ~2×10⁶×. A 0.5 m tide on a 6371 km radius is invisible. The envelope is scaled so the lunar crest is about 12% of the drawn Earth radius. True heights are on the chart and in the readout.
  4. Layout scale is not a map. Moon radius ratio is true (~0.27). Earth–Moon distance is compressed (true D/R ≈ 60). The Sun is a direction marker, not a distance.
  5. Real ports lag. In this model, highs occur when the pin lines up with the Earth–Moon axis (Moon “overhead” or “underfoot” in longitude). Real basins add hours of lag, friction, and amphidromic rotation. Continental shelves and funnels (Bay of Fundy, ~16 m) amplify far beyond 0.5 m. This page does not include that lag.
  6. Moon-in-equator default. Real lunar declination reaches about ±28.5° and produces diurnal or mixed tides at some latitudes. The declination slider is optional; it does not change the two-bulge mechanism.
  7. Frames. An equivalent story uses centrifugal force in the Earth–Moon barycenter frame. We use inertial free-fall / differential gravity because the classroom line “centrifugal is bigger on the far side” is often drawn incorrectly. Both residuals are the leftover after subtracting the acceleration of Earth’s center.
