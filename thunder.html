Why thunder comes after lightning

The flash and the bang are made at the same instant. A lightning channel heats a thin tube of air to roughly 30,000 K in a few millionths of a second. That air expands explosively and sends out a pressure wave: the thunder. Both then race toward you. Light covers a kilometre in about 3 microseconds; sound needs about 3 seconds. The gap you count is the distance, not a delay at the cloud.

Cause 01

Lightning heats air, and the air explodes

A return stroke dumps its energy into a channel a few centimetres wide in microseconds. The air there reaches roughly 30,000 K (about five times hotter than the Sun’s surface) and its pressure jumps to many times normal. It bursts outward as a shock wave, which weakens within metres into an ordinary sound wave: thunder.

Cause 02

Light and sound run a very uneven race

Light travels at c ≈ 300,000 km/s. Sound in air travels at v = 331.3·√(1 + T/273.15) m/s, about 343 m/s at 20 °C. Light is almost 900,000 times faster, so the flash reaches you essentially at once and the gap you hear is just the sound’s travel time.

Cause 03

Count the gap, get the distance

distance = v × delay. At 343 m/s that is about 3 s per kilometre or 5 s per mile: the flash-to-bang rule. The rumble lasts because the channel is kilometres long: sound from its near end arrives first, sound from the top of the channel arrives seconds later.

Myth, busted

“Thunder is clouds bumping” · “it happens later”

Clouds are mist; they can’t clang together. And the bang isn’t made late: if it were, the gap would be the same wherever you stood. It isn’t. Walk (or slide) closer and the gap shrinks toward zero; right next to a strike, flash and crack are simultaneous. Turn on myth mode to see the flat “made later” line fail against the real one.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Speed of sound. Ideal-gas dry air: v = 331.3·√(1 + T/273.15) m/s with T in °C (equivalently v = √(γRT/M)). It gives 343.2 m/s at 20 °C and 331.3 m/s at 0 °C. Humidity raises v by well under 1%, and wind adds or subtracts its own speed along the path; neither is modeled. Real air is colder aloft, which bends sound upward and is one reason thunder is rarely heard beyond about 15–25 km (the US National Weather Service quotes “about 10 miles”).
  2. Speed of light. c = 299,792,458 m/s (the refractive index of air, ≈ 1.0003, changes it by 0.03%). Light travel time is shown in microseconds; it is subtracted from the sound time to get the true flash-to-bang gap, although it only changes the answer in the sixth decimal place.
  3. Check. 1.000 km at 20 °C: t = 1000 / 343.2 = 2.914 s (about 2.9 s; the “3 s per km” rule overestimates the gap by ~3%, so it underestimates distance slightly). 1 mile (1609 m) takes 4.69 s, so “5 s per mile” is also a slight underestimate of distance. Both rules are for counting, not surveying.
  4. Channel temperature. Spectroscopic measurements of return strokes give peak temperatures of roughly 20,000–30,000 K (NOAA/NWS quotes about 50,000 °F), reached in microseconds, with channel pressures briefly around 10 atmospheres or more. The shock wave decays into ordinary sound within metres to tens of metres of the channel; the page treats thunder as ordinary sound from there on.
  5. Channel geometry and rumble. The drawn channel is a straight vertical line from the ground to a 3 km cloud base; the sound front is every point a distance v·t from that line (a capsule shape). Real channels are tortuous and branched, often run partly horizontal, and include several return strokes tens of milliseconds apart; these make the rumble longer and more irregular. The rumble duration shown, (√(d² + h²) − d)/v, is the spread for this idealized straight channel only.
  6. The sound. Filtered noise synthesized in your browser, only after you press Strike. Nearby strikes are given a sharper crack; distant ones are duller and quieter, because air absorbs high frequencies more strongly over distance. It is a sketch, not a recording.
  7. Safety. If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck (NWS “When thunder roars, go indoors”). Lightning can strike 10 miles or more from the storm. Don’t use the flash-to-bang count to decide it’s safe to stay outside.
  8. For teachers. Waves and their properties, speed of light vs sound (NGSS MS-PS4-1, MS-PS4-2, HS-PS4-1). Name the misconceptions: “thunder is clouds colliding” and “thunder happens after lightning.” A good extension: have students time a real storm and estimate its distance and speed of approach.
