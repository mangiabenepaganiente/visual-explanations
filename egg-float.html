Why old eggs float

A fresh egg is denser than water, about 1.08–1.09 g/cm³, so it sinks and lies on its side. But an eggshell is full of tiny pores. Day by day, water vapor escapes and air seeps in to fill the air cell at the blunt end. The shell doesn’t shrink, so the egg loses mass at nearly constant volume, and its density drops. First it tilts, then it stands on end, and once it’s less dense than water, it floats.

Cause 01

The shell leaks, slowly

An eggshell has thousands of microscopic pores so the chick can breathe. They also let water vapor out. On a warm counter an egg loses very roughly 0.25% of its mass a day. In the fridge it’s several times slower, about 0.04%.

Cause 02

Same size, less mass

The rigid shell keeps the volume fixed. As water leaves, air moves in and the air cell at the blunt end grows. Density is mass ÷ volume, so ρ = m/V falls steadily: 58 g in 53.5 cm³ is 1.085 g/cm³. Lose about 4.6 g and it’s below water.

Cause 03

Archimedes decides

Water pushes up with a force equal to the weight of the water the egg displaces, Fb = ρwater·V·g. While the egg is heavier than that, it sinks. The growing air cell makes the blunt end buoyant first, so the egg tilts and stands on end before it finally floats.

Myth, busted

“It floats because rot gas puffs it up” · “sinkers are safe”

The shell is rigid, so gas can’t inflate it. A floating egg is lighter, not bigger. The float test measures water loss, which mostly means age, not safety. A fresh, sinking egg can still carry Salmonella, and an old one may be fine. Go by storage, dates and smell.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Egg. A large egg: 58 g, fresh density 1.085 g/cm³ (published values cluster around 1.08–1.09), so its volume is 53.5 cm³. The shell is rigid, so volume stays constant. Fresh air cell 0.3 cm³, about 2.5 mm deep, within the USDA Grade AA limit of 1/8 inch (3.2 mm). Every gram of water lost is replaced by about 1 cm³ of air in the cell.
  2. Moisture loss rates (approximate, linear): counter (~22 °C) 0.25% of the starting mass per day, fridge (4 °C) 0.04% per day. Real rates depend on temperature, humidity, shell quality and whether the protective bloom was washed off (U.S. eggs are washed). Treat the day counts as rough. The order of events and the density threshold are the robust physics.
  3. Sink, tilt, stand, float. Floating is exact Archimedes: the egg floats when ρegg < ρliquid, with submerged fraction ρegg/ρliquid. The tilt and stand-on-end bands (Δρ = ρegg − ρliquid above 0.06 lies flat, 0.03–0.06 tilts, 0–0.03 stands) are an illustrative mapping of the familiar float-test chart. The real angle depends on the air cell’s size and position and where the yolk sits.
  4. Air cell depth treats the air cell as an end cap of an ellipsoid (egg half-length 28.5 mm): cap fraction (h/a)²(3 − h/a)/4, solved for h. USDA grades: AA ≤ 1/8 in, A ≤ 3/16 in, B larger.
  5. Salt water. Density rises roughly linearly with salt, from 0.998 (fresh, 20 °C) to 1.197 g/cm³ when saturated (26.4% NaCl by mass). We interpolate linearly, which is good to about 1%. A fresh egg floats once the brine passes about 1.085, around 12% salt.
  6. Safety. The float test indicates age and moisture loss, not bacteria. Salmonella can be inside an egg that sinks, and spoilage depends mostly on temperature and time. Keep eggs refrigerated, cook them thoroughly, and throw out any egg that smells off. If in doubt, throw it out.
  7. For teachers. Density and buoyancy (MS-PS1-2, HS-PS2), mass conservation, and diffusion through a porous membrane. Classic lab: float eggs of different ages in water and salt water, weigh them on a 0.01 g scale over a few weeks, and compare with the curves here.
