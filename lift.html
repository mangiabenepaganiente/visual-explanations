How do airplanes really fly?

The classroom story says air over the longer top path must meet the bottom air at the trailing edge, so it goes faster, so Bernoulli lifts the wing. NASA Glenn calls that incorrect. Parcels do not reunite. Lift is the upward reaction to downward momentum — and a pressure field that Bernoulli describes along a streamline. Both languages. Not a fight.

Cause 01

Parcels do not meet

Two tracers that start together at the leading edge do not reunite at the trailing edge. The upper parcel arrives first and is already downstream. There is no meeting rule to enforce.

Cause 02

Downwash is Newton

The wing throws air down. Lift is the upward reaction to that downward momentum. Watch the downwash arrow behind the trailing edge — turn the angle and the turn of the flow grows with it.

Cause 03

Bernoulli is the pressure field

Faster flow along a streamline sits at lower pressure. That is Bernoulli — a statement about the pressure field, not a race to the trailing edge. Newton and Bernoulli describe the same lift.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. This is an illustration, not Navier–Stokes and not CFD. The body is a unit-chord NACA 2412 (2% camber at 40% chord, 12% thickness). Streamlines are prescribed offset curves of that surface: horizontal inflow, body-following wrap, and a thin-airfoil-inspired downwash after the trailing edge of order α e^{−offset}. Speed along a surface pair is the flat-plate thin-airfoil jump u = U ± U α √((c−x)/x) (clamped near the leading-edge singularity). No viscosity, no boundary layer, no turbulence model.
  2. Lift coefficient cartoon. CL = 2π α with α in radians — the classical thin-airfoil / flat-plate result for small angle. At 5°, α = π/36 and CL = π²/18 ≈ 0.548 ≈ 0.55. Camber would shift the zero-lift angle (α_L0 ≈ −2° for a 2412); we do not include that, so the slider maps cleanly onto the textbook formula. Stall is not modeled past ~15°. The drawn line continues, but a real section peels and CL falls. Treat anything above ~15° as off-model.
  3. Live lift. L = CL · ½ ρ v² S with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ (ISA sea level), v from the slider, S = 16.2 m² set (Cessna 172-class). Circulation Γ = π U c α with c = 1.47 m. Kutta–Joukowski L = ρ U Γ b and b = S/c recover the same newton. The v slider changes the number, not the tracer animation speed (tracers are watchable, not to scale).
  4. Equal-transit overlay. Geometric surface lengths of the NACA 2412 are L_u/c ≈ 1.028 and L_l/c ≈ 1.013. The myth forces a common transit time T = c/U, so v_u = U L_u/c, v_l = U L_l/c, and a Bernoulli cartoon CL_fake = (L_u/c)² − (L_l/c)² ≈ 0.030. That number barely cares about angle of attack — it only knows path length — and it under-predicts Δv and lift versus the thin-airfoil illustration. The toggle does not change the honest CL or L; it only draws the forced-reunion pair and the fake readout.
  5. Newton and Bernoulli are both right. Downwash: the wing gives air downward momentum; the reaction is lift. Pressure: along a streamline, p + ½ρv² = const (steady, inviscid, incompressible), so the thin-airfoil speed field is a suction field on the upper surface. They are two descriptions of one force, not rival theories. Circulation is the bookkeeping that ties them together (Kutta condition at the trailing edge).
  6. NASA Glenn. “Incorrect Theory #1 — Equal Transit Time,” Beginner’s Guide to Aeronautics, NASA Glenn Research Center. The longer-path / must-meet story is explicitly flagged as wrong. Real upper-surface speeds are much higher than a path-length ratio can justify; parcels that split at the leading edge are found at different stations when one of them reaches the trailing edge (see also flow-visualization stills in the GRC notes and any PIV / smoke-line classroom film).
  7. 2D, inviscid, no induced drag. d’Alembert’s paradox: this illustration has lift and no profile drag. A finite wing adds trailing vortices and induced drag; a real Reynolds number adds skin friction and a wake. None of that is drawn. The net-force arrow is the lift vector (perpendicular to the freestream). Downwash angle is schematic.
