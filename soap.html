Why soap cleans grease

Water alone slides right over a greasy pan. Oil is nonpolar, water is polar, and water molecules would rather stick to each other than to oil. A soap molecule is two-faced. It has a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. At low concentration the molecules just line the surfaces. Above the critical micelle concentration (CMC, 8.2 mM for the classic soap molecule sodium dodecyl sulfate at 25 °C) they cluster into micelles, balls with the tails tucked inside. Scrubbing tears grease into droplets, the micelles wrap them in an oily core, and the rinse water carries them off. Nothing is dissolved or destroyed. The grease is packaged and moved.

Try thisDrag Soap concentration past the dashed CMC line (about 8 mM). Notice that the surface tension stops falling at that point and micelles appear. Only then does most of the grease lift off the plate.

Cause 01

Oil and water don’t mix

Water molecules are polar and hold onto each other with hydrogen bonds. Oil molecules are long nonpolar chains that can’t join in. Pushing them apart costs energy, so water beads up and slides past grease. Hot water and scrubbing help a little, but the oil just settles back onto the plate.

Cause 02

A molecule with two personalities

Soap is amphiphilic. It has a charged head (for SDS, –OSO₃⁻ Na⁺) that water loves and a 12-carbon tail that hides from water. The molecules crowd onto any oil–water or air–water surface, tails out of the water. That is why surface tension falls from 72 to about 38 mN/m.

Cause 03

Micelles: oily pockets you can rinse

Once the surfaces are full, at the CMC, extra molecules team up into micelles of about 60, tails inward. That core is a tiny drop of oily space. Grease torn loose by scrubbing ends up inside or coated by a soap shell, and the charged heads keep the droplets from merging back onto the plate. Rinsing flushes them away.

Myth, busted

“Soap dissolves grease”

Soap doesn’t break grease down chemically. Every oil molecule that leaves the plate still exists, carried off in a micelle or a soap-coated droplet. Toggle the myth and count: nothing is destroyed, only moved. It also isn’t brute strength: below the CMC, soap barely lifts any grease.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Which soap. All numbers use sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), the textbook model surfactant. Its CMC is 8.2 mM at 25 °C (Mukerjee & Mysels, Critical Micelle Concentrations of Aqueous Surfactant Systems, NSRDS-NBS 36, 1971). The aggregation number is about 62 and the micelle diameter about 4 nm. Bar soap (sodium stearate and oleate) and dish detergents (blends of LAS, SLES, and nonionics) have different CMCs, often lower, but the same mechanism.
  2. Micelle fraction (pseudo-phase model). Free single molecules (monomers) top out at the CMC, and everything above it goes into micelles. So the fraction in micelles is (C − CMC)/C, which is 50% at 2× CMC. The real transition is slightly rounded over a narrow range.
  3. Surface tension. Pure water uses the IAPWS equation (71.97 mN/m at 25 °C). Below the CMC the soap lowers it following the Szyszkowski isotherm γ = γ₀ − n·RTΓmax ln(1 + K·C), with n = 2 for an ionic surfactant without added salt, Γmax = 3.4 µmol/m², and K chosen so γ ≈ 38.5 mN/m at the CMC at 25 °C. That gives about 62 mN/m at 1 mM, close to measured SDS curves. Measured SDS values at the CMC are roughly 35–40 mN/m. Above the CMC, γ stays flat. That flat break is how the CMC is measured.
  4. Temperature (approximate). SDS’s CMC has a shallow minimum near room temperature (fit: ≈ 8.4 mM at 10 °C, 9.7 mM at 60 °C). Below SDS’s Krafft point (≈ 16 °C), the surfactant crystallizes out before it can reach the CMC, so no micelles form. Commercial detergents are blended to avoid this. Warm water also softens solid fats like butter or lard, modeled as a smooth step around 32 °C.
  5. Cleaning kinetics are a simplified, labeled model. Grease is split into “on the plate”, “lifted into the water”, and “rinsed away”. Scrubbing lifts it at a rate that grows with agitation, warmth, monomers (a little) and micelles (a lot). Lifted oil settles back at a rate that micelles suppress. Each Rinse flushes whatever is lifted. The rate constants are tuned for illustration, not taken from measurements. Real cleaning also depends on grease type, the surface, water hardness (calcium makes soap scum), and enzymes or builders in detergents.
  6. Germs. Handwashing works mainly by this same lift-and-rinse. Soap can also break apart the fatty envelope of some viruses, but it is not a disinfectant in the way bleach or alcohol are. Scrub for about 20 seconds.
  7. For teachers. Covers polarity, intermolecular forces, structure and function of molecules (NGSS HS-PS1-3, MS-PS1-2; AP Chemistry intermolecular forces). Misconceptions to name: “soap dissolves or destroys grease”, “more soap is always better” (beyond a few × CMC, returns diminish), and “hot water alone cleans”.
