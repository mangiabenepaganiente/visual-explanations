Why bread rises

Yeast cells don’t swell up, and the yeast isn’t the air. Each cell is a tiny sugar-eating factory. It ferments sugar into carbon dioxide and alcohol: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂. The CO₂ gas seeps into bubbles that kneading left in the dough. Gluten, a stretchy protein network, holds those bubbles in like millions of tiny balloons.

Cause 01

Yeast makes gas from sugar

Without much oxygen, yeast ferments glucose (and maltose from starch) into ethanol and CO₂. By mass, 180 g of glucose → 88 g of CO₂, which is 2 mol, or about 49 L of gas at 25 °C and 1 atm (V = nRT/P). A few grams of sugar are enough to inflate a loaf.

Cause 02

Gluten traps the bubbles

Kneading wheat flour with water builds gluten, a stretchy protein film. It also traps tiny air pockets that seed bubbles. CO₂ diffuses into those pockets and inflates them. Strong bread flour holds more gas and stretches further. Weak cake flour leaks and tears sooner.

Cause 03

Warm speeds it — until it kills

Fermentation is enzyme chemistry, so it speeds up with warmth to a peak near 35–38 °C. Hotter than ~45 °C it falters, and at 55–60 °C yeast dies within minutes. In the oven the last burst of gas plus expansion and steam gives oven spring, until the crumb sets near 75 °C.

Myth, busted

“The yeast puffs up”

A yeast cell is about 5 µm across and stays that size. All the yeast in a loaf is about a teaspoon (~7 mL). A risen loaf holds hundreds of mL of gas. Yeast isn’t the air, either: the bubbles are mostly CO₂ the yeast made. Turn on the myth toggle to see how little the cells themselves could account for.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Chemistry is exact, growth is simplified. The mole bookkeeping is exact: each mole of glucose-equivalent sugar gives 2 mol CO₂ and 2 mol ethanol (M = 180.16, 44.01, 46.07 g/mol). Gas volume uses the ideal-gas law V = nRT/P with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and P = 1 atm. Check: 180 g glucose → 88.0 g CO₂ = 2.00 mol → 48.9 L at 25 °C. Bubbles also hold saturated water vapour (Buck 1996 formula), which is included.
  2. Temperature activity curve (a labeled simplification). Gas-production rate × the cardinal-temperature model of Rosso et al. (1993): zero below Tmin = 2 °C, peak at Topt = 37 °C, zero at Tmax = 52 °C. Heat death is first-order with a decimal time D = 3 min at 55 °C, z = 6 °C (D shrinks 10× per 6 °C hotter, applied above 40 °C). Real strains, doughs and published values vary. These round numbers give the right shape, not strain-specific data.
  3. Rate calibration. The peak rate is set so a standard dough (500 g flour, 325 g water, 7 g instant yeast, 27 °C) roughly doubles in about an hour, a common baking rule of thumb. It scales with yeast mass. Sugar supply uses a Monod-style limit, with ~15 g of fermentable sugar from the flour itself (free sugars plus amylase working on starch, lumped together) plus any added sugar. Above roughly 5–10% sugar (baker’s %) the syrup osmotically slows yeast. A simple penalty 1/(1 + (sugar%/14)³) models that, which is why very sweet doughs rise slowly.
  4. Where the CO₂ goes. The first CO₂ dissolves in the dough water (Henry’s law, ~0.034 mol/L·atm at 25 °C, less when warm) before bubbles grow. After that, a flour-dependent fraction is retained by gluten (cake 45%, all-purpose 60%, bread 75%) and the rest leaks out. Each flour has a maximum stretch (2.2×, 2.8×, 3.4× the starting volume). Past it the gluten tears, gas escapes and the dough slumps (over-proofed).
  5. Bake. The dough warms toward 98 °C (time constant 6 min). Gas expands with T (Charles’s law) and water-vapour pressure climbs steeply, while the dying yeast adds a last burst. The crumb sets at 75 °C and the volume freezes. Oven spring is capped at 1.15× the flour’s proofing stretch limit (warm dough stretches further before it sets). That cap is a simplification. Real ovens add crust formation, ethanol boil-off and gelatinizing starch, which are not modeled.
  6. The dough is at the slider temperature. Real dough lags its surroundings and warms a little from fermentation. Salt (2%) is assumed, and its mild slowing effect is folded into the calibration. The on-screen “yeast cells” are drawn huge so you can see them. Bubbles are schematic.
  7. For teachers. Chemical reactions and conservation of atoms (NGSS MS-PS1-2, MS-PS1-5), gas laws (HS-PS1), and living cells’ energy use (MS-LS1-7). Pairs with the balloon-on-a-bottle yeast demo: warm vs cold vs hot water. Name the misconception: “the yeast swells” / “yeast is air.”
