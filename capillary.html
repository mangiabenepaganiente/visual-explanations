Why water climbs a paper towel

A paper towel is not a straw and nothing is sucking. It is a tangle of cellulose fibers with narrow gaps between them. Water sticks to cellulose (adhesion) and its surface acts like a stretched skin (surface tension), so it pulls itself up each gap until the weight of the lifted water balances that pull. The narrower the gap, the higher it climbs.

Cause 01

Adhesion: water likes cellulose

Water molecules are attracted to the –OH groups on cellulose (and glass) more than to each other, so the liquid creeps up the wall. The contact angle θ measures it: small θ = wets well, θ > 90° = beads up.

Cause 02

Surface tension pulls the column

The curved surface (meniscus) acts like a stretched skin hooked to the wall. Around the rim it pulls up with force 2πr·γ·cosθ. That lowers the pressure just under the meniscus by 2γcosθ/r, and the outside air pressure pushes liquid up to fill in.

Cause 03

Gravity sets the finish line

The column stops when its weight πr²ρgh equals the pull. Solve and you get Jurin’s law h = 2γcosθ/(ρgr). The pull grows with r, but the weight grows with r², so thinner pores lift higher.

Myth, busted

“The towel sucks it up”

Suction would lift every pore to the same height, and it would lift mercury too. Real pores do neither. Thin pores win, wax paper and mercury push the liquid down, and an oily surface stops the climb. That is the fingerprint of a surface force, not a pump.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Jurin’s law (equilibrium height). For a vertical circular tube of radius r: h = 2γ·cosθ / (ρ·g·r), from balancing the surface-tension pull 2πrγcosθ with the column weight πr²ρgh. Check: water (γ = 0.072 N/m, ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³), θ = 0°, r = 0.1 mm gives h ≈ 0.147 m. g = 9.80665 m/s². It ignores the small volume of liquid in the meniscus itself (matters only when r is comparable to h).
  2. Rise with time: Lucas–Washburn with gravity. Poiseuille flow driven by the capillary pressure minus the column’s hydrostatic pressure: dh/dt = r²/(8ηh) · (2γcosθ/r − ρgh). Without gravity this is the classic h² = γ·r·cosθ·t / (2η). The page uses the exact implicit solution t(h) = (2/B)·(−h − H·ln(1 − h/H)) with H = Jurin height and B = ρgr²/(4η), inverted numerically — no time-stepping error. Inertia at the very first instant and the dynamic (moving) contact angle are neglected.
  3. Why thin tubes start slower but finish higher. Early on, Washburn gives h² ∝ r·t, so the wide tube leads. Later, gravity caps each tube at H ∝ 1/r and the thin tube overtakes. Watch the curves cross on the chart.
  4. Liquids (≈ 20–25 °C). Water γ 0.0720 N/m, ρ 997 kg/m³, η 0.89 mPa·s. Ethanol 0.0220, 789, 1.08. Olive oil ≈ 0.032, 911, ≈ 80 (varies by oil). Glycerol 0.0634, 1261, ≈ 934. Mercury 0.485, 13 534, 1.53, with θ ≈ 140° on glass (non-wetting → capillary depression).
  5. Contact angles are pair-specific. The material presets are rough values for water (clean glass ≈ 0°, cellulose ≈ 20°, acrylic ≈ 70°, paraffin wax ≈ 108°). Real angles vary with cleanliness, roughness, and whether the liquid is advancing or receding. Alcohols and oils wet cellulose more readily than water, so try a smaller θ for them. Choosing mercury sets θ = 140°.
  6. A real paper towel is not a bundle of tubes. It is an irregular, swelling network of fibers with a spread of pore sizes (tens of µm). Effective-radius Jurin estimates suggest tens of centimetres to metres, but real strips stall far lower. Evaporation from the huge wet surface, fibers swelling, the widest connected pores, and the painfully slow late-stage Washburn crawl all limit it. Treat the tubes as an idealized model of one gap, not a towel prediction. Tube widths on screen are not to scale.
  7. For teachers. Pairs with the celery / food-colouring and glass-capillary demos (NGSS MS-PS1 structure and properties of matter; HS-PS2 forces). Name the misconception: “suction” implies a pressure pump that would lift any liquid equally and ignore pore size and wetting.
