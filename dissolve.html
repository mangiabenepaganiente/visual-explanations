Sugar doesn’t disappear when it dissolves

Stir sugar into water and the crystals vanish. Students conclude the sugar is gone — destroyed, evaporated, or somehow lighter. It is not. Dissolving is a physical change: sucrose molecules leave the crystal lattice and disperse among water molecules. In a closed system the scale reading stays put.

Try thisHit Play and watch crystals become particles. Keep an eye on the scale — it should not drop. Then flip the myth chip and see the fake “vanishing mass” story.

Cause 01

Physical, not chemical

Sucrose (C12H22O11) does not break into new substances when it dissolves. Water surrounds the molecules and pulls them from the crystal. Same formula, new arrangement — a solution, not a reaction product.

Cause 02

Molecules disperse

What “vanishes” is the crystal — a lattice big enough to scatter light. The sugar units are still there, too small to see as grains, mixed through the water. Taste and a closed-system weighing both prove it.

Cause 03

Mass is conserved

In a closed beaker (no splash-out, no evaporation modeled here): m_water + m_sugar = m_solution. The scale reads the same before and after. “Gone” would require open-system loss.

Myth, busted

“It’s gone / lighter”

Invisible ≠ destroyed. Dissolved sugar is still sugar. Toggle the myth chip: the fake scale drops as crystals clear. The true readout never does. That drop is the misconception drawn in neon.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Conservation of mass. For a closed system with no chemical reaction that exchanges mass with the surroundings: m_before = m_water + m_sugar and m_after = m_solution = m_water + m_sugar. The live scale uses that identity. Displayed grams are rounded to 0.1 g; the underlying floats stay exact so Δm is zero within rounding.
  2. Physical change. Ordinary table sugar in water is a molecular solution of sucrose. No covalent bonds in C12H22O11 are broken by dissolving; hydration and lattice breakup are intermolecular. (Ionic salts dissolve as ions — still a physical change in the classroom sense, different particle cartoon. This page models sucrose.)
  3. Particle model. N_SUGAR = 48 cartoon units stand in for ~1.8×1022 real sucrose molecules in 10 g. Crystal clusters and free Brownian dots are the same units relocated. Particle count is conserved at every progress value: n_crystal + n_free = N_SUGAR.
  4. Progress parameter. Slider p ∈ [0, 1] is the dissolved fraction of sugar mass / units. Crystal mass remaining = (1 − p) · m_sugar; dissolved mass = p · m_sugar. Play advances p at a fixed rate; Stir adds a swirl velocity field to free particles only.
  5. Myth chip. When “Myth: sugar gone” is on, the drawn scale displays m_water + (1 − p) · m_sugar — the false story that dissolved sugar leaves the system. True mass readouts and the before/after chart stay at 110.0 g. The chip is a teaching overlay, not physics.
  6. What we omit. Evaporation, splash-out, temperature, solubility limits (~200 g sucrose / 100 mL water at 20 °C — 10 g in 100 g water is well undersaturated), density rise of the solution, and osmotic effects. The beaker glass tare is omitted so the scale shows contents only.
  7. For teachers. Pairs with a sealed-bottle weigh-before/weigh-after lab. NGSS MS-PS1-2 / MS-PS1-5 (conservation of mass; physical vs chemical). Name the misconception out loud: “If I can’t see the crystals, the sugar is gone.”
