Why a ceiling fan cools you, not the room

A fan doesn’t make cold air. It just pushes the air that’s already there, and its motor turns every watt it draws into heat, so an empty room with a fan running gets very slightly warmer. What the moving air does is peel away the warm, humid layer that clings to your skin. That speeds up convection and sweat evaporation, so your body loses heat faster. You feel cooler; the thermometer doesn’t.

Cause 01

Moving air thins the warm blanket

Still air next to your skin warms up and acts like insulation. Moving air scrapes that layer away, so fresh, cooler air touches the skin. The convective coefficient climbs roughly as hc ≈ 8.3·v0.6 W/m²K, from about 3 in still air to about 8 at 1 m/s.

Cause 02

…and carries sweat vapour away

Evaporation needs somewhere for the vapour to go. A breeze replaces the damp air at the skin with drier room air, so sweat evaporates faster and each gram takes about 2,400 J with it. Humid air leaves less room for vapour, which is why a fan helps less on muggy days.

Cause 03

The motor’s watts become heat

Energy is conserved. Every joule the fan draws ends up as heat in the room: motor losses warm the housing, and the air’s kinetic energy is soon dissipated by friction. A 45 W fan warms a room like a 45 W heater. It can only move heat around, never remove it.

Myth, busted

“The fan cools the room”

If fans cooled rooms, leaving one on in an empty room would help. It doesn’t: with nobody there, a fan just adds its own heat. The cooling is a rate effect on bodies (and wet things), not a temperature change of the air. That’s why energy guides say turn fans off when you leave.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Skin heat loss (simplified ASHRAE two-path model). A seated adult, body surface A = 1.8 m², mean skin temperature held at 34 °C, light summer clothing (0.5 clo, Rcl = 0.0775 m²K/W, clothing area factor 1.1). Dry loss Q = A·(Tsk − Ta)/(Rcl + 1/(fcl(hc + hr))) with hr = 4.7 W/m²K and walls at air temperature. Convection: hc = 8.3·v0.6 for 0.2–4 m/s and 3.1 W/m²K in still air (ASHRAE Handbook—Fundamentals, ch. 9, seated person).
  2. Evaporation. E = A·w·(psk,s − pa)/(Re,cl + 1/(fcl·he)), with the Lewis relation he = 16.5·hc W/m²kPa, clothing vapour resistance from permeability index 0.34, saturation pressure from the Magnus formula (5.32 kPa at 34 °C). Skin wettedness w is a simple assumption, not a thermoregulation model: 0.06 (insensible diffusion) up to 26 °C, rising 0.02 per °C to a cap of 0.30.
  3. “Feels like”. The still-air (0.1 m/s) temperature that gives the same total skin heat loss, holding vapour pressure and wettedness fixed. At 28 °C, 50% RH, 0.8 m/s it gives about 2.6 °C of cooling, in line with the roughly 1 °C per 0.3 m/s at modest speeds and the ~2–3 °C cooling allowances in ASHRAE Standard 55’s elevated-air-speed method (which uses the fuller SET model; above ~1.2 m/s most people find the draft unpleasant). Because skin temperature and sweating are held fixed, the model overstates cooling at high speed. Above ~35 °C the air is hotter than skin: the fan then adds heat by convection and only evaporation helps; in very hot, humid air a fan can make things worse.
  4. Room energy balance. One well-mixed lump: C·dT/dt = Pfan + Pperson − UA·(T − Tstart), solved exactly: ΔT(t) = (P/UA)(1 − e−t·UA/C). C = 400 kJ/K (41.6 m³ of air is only ~50 kJ/K; the rest stands in for furniture and wall surfaces that warm along with it) and UA = 60 W/K for leakage to the rest of the house and outdoors, both order-of-magnitude guesses for a closed 4 × 4 m bedroom. The person adds about 70 W of sensible heat (of ~100 W metabolism; the rest leaves as water vapour). A running air conditioner would hide the rise by working slightly harder instead.
  5. Where the fan’s power goes. All electrical input ends up as heat in the room, including the air’s kinetic energy (dissipated by turbulence within seconds). The fan’s share of the eventual rise is Pfan/UA, under 1 °C here. Real ceiling fans draw roughly 30–75 W on high (efficient DC-motor fans much less); fan speed and power are separate sliders so you can explore, though in a real fan they rise together.
  6. Sources. ASHRAE Handbook—Fundamentals (2021), ch. 9 Thermal Comfort; ASHRAE Standard 55-2020, elevated air speed; U.S. Department of Energy, Fans for Cooling (“a ceiling fan will allow you to raise the thermostat setting about 4 °F with no reduction in comfort… turn off ceiling fans when you leave a room”).
  7. For teachers. Energy transfer and conservation (NGSS MS-PS3-3, MS-PS3-4, HS-PS3-1). Name the misconception: “a fan cools the air.” Classic check: put a thermometer in front of a fan. A dry thermometer doesn’t change; a wet one (wrapped in damp cloth) drops, because it can evaporate like skin.
