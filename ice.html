Why ice floats (and why that saves lakes)

Almost every solid sinks in its own melt. Ice does not. Water freezes into an open hexagonal lattice (ice Ih) that is about 9% roomier than the liquid — so a lid of ice forms on lakes, and life keeps a liquid refuge underneath. It is not because “cold things float.”

Cause 01

Hydrogen-bond geometry

Each H2O can make four hydrogen bonds. In ice Ih those bonds lock a tetrahedral network into open hexagonal channels. The holes are the story — the solid is roomier than the liquid.

Cause 02

Density decides floating

Ice Ih is 916.7 kg/m³ at 0 °C. Liquid water is 999.8 kg/m³ at 0 °C and peaks near 1000 at 3.98 °C. Submerged fraction is ρice/ρwater ≈ 0.917, so about 8% of an iceberg sits above the waterline.

Cause 03

Lakes freeze from the top

The floating lid insulates. Water under the ice stays liquid, and the densest 4 °C water sinks to the bed. Fish, insects, and plants keep a winter refuge. Toggle “If ice sank” to see the counterfactual.

Myth, busted

Not “because it’s cold”

Cold liquid water is denser than warm water down to 4 °C — it sinks. Ice floats because of the open lattice, not the thermometer. Most solids are denser than their melt. Water is the classroom exception.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Ice density. Ordinary ice Ih at 0 °C is taken as ρ_ice = 916.7 kg/m³ (0.9167 g/cm³), the usual CRC / handbook value for air-free ice at atmospheric pressure. Ice does get slightly denser as it cools further (linear expansion ~50×10⁻⁶ /K); that shift is a few kg/m³ and is omitted — the lattice-versus-liquid gap is ~83 kg/m³.
  2. Liquid density (CIPM-2001 / Tanaka). Air-free Standard Mean Ocean Water at 101 325 Pa, valid 0–40 °C: ρ(t) = a₅ [1 − (t + a₁)² (t + a₂) / (a₃ (t + a₄))] with a₁ = −3.983035 °C, a₂ = 301.797 °C, a₃ = 522528.9 °C², a₄ = 69.34881 °C, a₅ = 999.974950 kg/m³ (Tanaka, Girard, Davis, Peuto & Bignell, Metrologia 38, 301–309, 2001; CIPM recommended table). This gives ρ(0 °C) ≈ 999.84, a maximum ρ(3.98 °C) ≈ 999.975, and ρ(20 °C) ≈ 998.21 kg/m³. Below 0 °C the remaining lake water is treated as 0 °C (the ice–water interface). Supercooled-water density is not modeled.
  3. Buoyancy. For an ice body in hydrostatic equilibrium, submerged volume fraction = ρ_ice / ρ_water(T). At 0 °C that is 916.7 / 999.84 ≈ 0.917, so ~8.3% sits above the waterline. The live cube uses that ratio at the slider temperature (ice cubes in a drink). Real icebergs include trapped air and snow; the drawn cube is solid ice Ih.
  4. Expansion. Volume change on freezing 0 °C water is ρ_water(0)/ρ_ice − 1 ≈ 9.07%. The classroom “about 9%” is this number, not a new fit.
  5. Lattice drawing is a cartoon. The ice panel is a 2-D honeycomb — the usual classroom view looking down the c-axis of ice Ih, where hexagonal rings and empty channels are visible. Real ice is a 3-D tetrahedral network (each oxygen has four neighbors). The liquid panel is a tighter, disordered 2-D packing with flickering near-neighbor bonds, not a molecular-dynamics trajectory. Molecule counts are chosen so the liquid side is ~9% denser in the same box.
  6. Lake is schematic. Ice thickness is not a heat-diffusion model. In the real toggle, the lid stays thin on purpose: ice is a poor conductor, so growth slows. In the sinking toggle, ice is allowed to pile until the basin is nearly solid — the counterfactual that makes the ecological point. Real lakes also mix, freeze from the edges, and can go anoxic under a long ice cover; those are not drawn. Fish are icons.
  7. The 4 °C anomaly is small. The bump from 0 °C to 3.98 °C is only ~0.13 kg/m³. The meter zooms the water curve so you can see it; the ice–water gap (~83 kg/m³) is a different scale, shown on the bars and the cube. Both are real.
  8. For teachers. Density, phase change, and a winter-lakes / ecosystems hook. Pairs with a middle-school density lab (NGSS MS-PS1 structure and properties of matter; MS-ESS2 Earth systems). The misconception to name out loud: solids are always denser than their liquids, or ice floats “because it is cold.”
