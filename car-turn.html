Why you’re not thrown outward in a turn

When a car swerves left you slam into the right door, and it feels like a force flung you outward. Watched from the roadside, nothing did. Your body just keeps going straight (Newton’s first law) while the car turns under you. The door then pushes you inward with F = m·v²/r. The car’s own inward push comes from tire friction, which tops out at μ·m·g. Ask for more and the car skids.

Try thisPress Remove the door in Ground view. Notice the passenger doesn’t fly sideways. They leave along a straight line tangent to the curve while the car turns away.

Cause 01

Your body keeps going straight

Newton’s first law: with no sideways force, you keep your velocity. At the start of the turn the car swings left and you don’t, so the right side of the car comes toward you. You meet the door. Nothing pulled you right.

Cause 02

The door pushes you inward

To follow a curve of radius r at speed v, something must push you toward the center with F = m·v²/r. For you that’s the door, seat, and belt. At 25 m/s on a 50 m curve that’s 12.5 m/s² ≈ 1.27 g, or 875 N on a 70 kg rider. That is more than dry tires can give (about 0.9 g), so the car would skid.

Cause 03

Tires supply the car’s inward force

The road pushes the car sideways through tire friction, at most μ·m·g on a flat road. So the fastest you can take a curve is vmax = √(μ·g·r). Mass cancels. Beyond that the tires slide and the car runs wide.

Myth, busted

“Centrifugal force throws you outward”

In the ground frame there is no outward force on you at all. Toggle the myth and remove the door: the myth predicts you fly straight out sideways (red), but you really leave along the tangent (blue). “Centrifugal force” is a fictitious force: bookkeeping that appears only if you insist on describing things from inside the turning car.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Uniform circular motion. Required inward (centripetal) acceleration a = v²/r, with g = 9.80665 m/s² for the “g” readout. The car is a 1500 kg point for the force budget. The passenger is 70 kg. The force the car must give the passenger is m·a, whatever the car is actually doing.
  2. Tire friction (Coulomb model). On a flat, unbanked road the most sideways force the tires can give is μs·m·g. Peak values used (typical ranges, not for any specific tire): dry asphalt 0.9 (0.8–1.0), wet asphalt 0.6 (0.4–0.7), packed snow 0.25 (0.2–0.3), ice 0.1 (0.05–0.15). When the needed force exceeds this, the car slides and gets only the sliding value μk ≈ 0.75·μs, so it follows a wider circle of radius v²/(μk·g). The skid is drawn at constant speed. Real skids also slow the car and often spin it. ABS, stability control, load transfer, tire slip angles, and banked curves are all ignored.
  3. Passenger. We treat the seat as slippery and ignore the seatbelt, so the door supplies all of the passenger’s inward force. In a real car the seat friction and belt share that load. When the door “vanishes” the passenger becomes a free body. Air drag and seat friction are ignored, so the path is a straight line at the passenger’s velocity at that instant.
  4. Passenger’s view. This frame turns with the car, so it is non-inertial. To make Newton’s laws work in it you must add a fictitious centrifugal force m·ω²·r pointing outward (plus a Coriolis term for anything moving in that frame). The straight tangent path from the ground view is exactly what shows up in this frame as the passenger curving outward and backward. Same motion, two descriptions. The forces that are real, the ones with an agent doing the pushing, are the inward ones.
  5. Drawing scale. In Ground view the road and paths are to scale, but the car is enlarged (shown in the header) so you can see it on big curves. The free-flight line is drawn from the enlarged seat position.
  6. For teachers. Covers Newton’s first and second laws and centripetal force (NGSS HS-PS2-1). The FCI-style misconception is that an object released from circular motion flies outward or keeps curving. The ground-view release shows the straight tangent path.
