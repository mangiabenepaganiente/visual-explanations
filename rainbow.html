Why rainbows are circles — and why you only see an arc

The rainbow is not a physical colored arch sitting in a place, and the rain is not dyed. Each drop sends a given color to your eye at a nearly fixed scattering angle — about 42° for red, 40° for violet. Those drops lie on a cone with you at the tip. Ground clips the bottom; from a plane you can see a circle.

Cause 01

One drop, one angle

Sunlight enters a spherical drop, reflects once off the back, and leaves. Descartes’ minimum-deviation ray for red water is ~42.3° from the anti-solar direction. Violet, with a slightly higher n, comes out ~40.6° — so violet sits inside red.

Cause 02

A cone of drops

Every drop that can send that color to your eye lies at the same angle from the anti-solar axis. The locus is a cone with you at the tip. On the sky that cone is a circle around the anti-Sun.

Cause 03

Ground clips the circle

From the ground you cannot look through dirt, so you only get the part of the circle above the horizon — an arc. Raise the observer (a hill, then a plane) and more of the cone is in view. From the air the circle can close.

Myth, busted

You cannot “go to the end”

Walk toward the bow: it walks with you. The rainbow is not a place, and the rain is not colored. It is a direction — a set of drops that happen to send 42° light into this pair of eyes.

Model, constants, caveats

  1. Descartes / geometric optics. A spherical drop, one internal reflection (primary, k = 1). Incidence i and refraction r obey Snell sin i = n sin r with n_air = 1. The scattering angle from the anti-solar direction is θ = 4r − 2i. The rainbow is the stationary (minimum-deviation) ray: dθ/di = 0 gives cos i = √((n² − 1) / (k(k+2))), so for the primary cos i = √((n² − 1)/3). Published classroom values: θ_r ≈ 42.3° (red 650 nm), θ_v ≈ 40.6° (violet 400 nm). Descartes, Les Météores (1637); Walker, Am. J. Phys. 44, 421 (1976).
  2. Cauchy n(water). n(λ) = 1.32425 + 3050 / λ² with λ in nm. This two-term Cauchy fit is tuned so the Descartes primary lands on the published angles (red 42.3°, violet 40.6°) and so n(589 nm) ≈ 1.333 (the usual sodium-D classroom index). It is not a full Sellmeier water model; temperature and dissolved salt shift n at the 0.001 level and the bow by a few tenths of a degree.
  3. Secondary bow. Two internal reflections (k = 2): cos i = √((n² − 1)/8) and angular radius from the anti-Sun θ₂ = 180° + 2i − 6r ≈ 51°. Extra reflection reverses the color order (red inside, violet outside) and dims the bow. Alexander’s dark band is the sky between the two caustics — not a cloud shadow.
  4. Cone and clipping. Anti-solar elevation = −α if the Sun is at elevation α. Bow-top elevation ≈ θ − α (flat-sky). When α > θ the whole primary is below the astronomical horizon. Ground-level clip elevation is 0°. Height uses a pedagogical look-down model clip = −90° · h / (h + 1800 m) so a hill shows a sliver of the lower bow and a 10 km airplane can close the circle on a cloud deck. Real geometric horizon dip at 10 km is only ~3°; the full circle is a “rain or cloud around / below you” effect, not the dip of the sea horizon.
  5. It moves when you move. The cone is attached to your eye and the anti-solar axis. Walking translates the cone. There is no pot of gold and no “end” you can reach. Two observers a few meters apart have slightly different rainbows.
  6. What this page leaves out. Drops are treated as spheres (large-drop geometric optics). Airy’s interference theory — supernumerary fringes inside the primary — is omitted. Polarization, Mie scattering for small drops, and the faint tertiary bows are omitted. The sky map uses spherical directions for the circle and a linear elevation/azimuth projection (fine near the horizon; footnoted as a classroom map, not a photograph). Drawings are not to scale: the drop, the cone, and the sky dome are three different pictures of one angle.
